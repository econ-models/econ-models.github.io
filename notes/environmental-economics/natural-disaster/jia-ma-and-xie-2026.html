Model Setting

The paper asks how flood risk, as opposed to realized flood events, shapes where firms locate and how much people work, and what this implies for aggregate output. Using county-level and zip-code-level US data for 1998-2018, the authors document two facts: (i) a rise in flood risk has a large, negative long-run effect on firm entry, employment and output; (ii) realized floods reduce output in the year they occur but have little effect on firm entry or employment. Flood risk is measured from digitized FEMA maps: the historic Q3 Flood Insurance Rate Maps for 1998 and the National Flood Hazard Layer for 2018. The measure is the share of a county's (or zip code's) land inside Special Flood Hazard Zones, which are areas with at least a 1 percent annual chance of inundation. On average this share rose by 6 percentage points between 1998 and 2018, and by 19 percentage points at the ninetieth percentile of county changes. Flood events come from the Dartmouth Flood Observatory (DFO). Firm entry, exit and employment come from the Census Business Dynamics Statistics, prime-age population from the Census, county real GDP from the BEA, and zip-code outcomes from the Zip Codes Business Patterns. The facts motivate a quantitative spatial model with Krugman (1980) free entry and McFadden (1978) location choice. Firms and workers make long-run decisions before floods are realized and anticipate flood risk; realized floods lower productivity and amenities and destroy some firms. In the calibrated model, flood risk lowered US aggregate output by 0.53 percent in 2018. Direct damages account for 23 percent of this loss and long-run adjustment by firms and workers for 77 percent.

Each region is a county, and the calibration targets 2018; the counties used account for 96 percent of US GDP. The elasticity is σ=5\sigma=5σ=5, the mean trade estimate in Head and Mayer (2014), which implies a markup of 0.25. Calibration: Internal Parameters and Identification

The model is calibrated in levels rather than with exact hat algebra, for consistency with the trade extension, where bilateral flows are unobserved. Exact hat algebra in the baseline gives very similar results. National GDP, population and firm count are normalized to one. Counterfactual 1: Aggregate Effects of Flood Risk in 2018

The baseline equilibrium is compared with one in which rm=0r_m=0rm​=0 for all mmm. Flood risk lowers aggregate output by 0.53 percent, employment by 0.33 percent, firm entry by 0.30 percent, firm exits by 0.15 percent and welfare by 0.52 percent. The decomposition lets channels adjust one at a time. First, direct damages alone are computed holding labor supply and population fixed. Then population shares, labor supply and the number of varieties move in turn to their no-risk levels. Direct damages account for 0.12 percent of output, close to the FEMA estimate of about 17 billion dollars per year in 2010-2018, roughly 0.1 percent of GDP. That is only 23 percent of the total. Labor relocation accounts for 0.02 percent, because inflows and outflows offset. Lower labor supply accounts for 0.31 percent (58 percent of the loss), and fewer varieties for 0.08 percent (15 percent). Ignoring the long-run adjustment of firms and workers therefore greatly understates the loss.

Counterfactual 2: Distributional Effects

The national loss hides large heterogeneity. The top 5 percent and top 1 percent of counties ranked by output loss lose 9.1 percent and 15.9 percent of output; examples include coastal places such as Cape May, New Jersey. Direct damages concentrate in southern and eastern, especially coastal, counties. In the top 1 percent of counties population falls by 7.1 percent, labor supply per person by 5.4 percent and the number of firms by 12.5 percent. Mildly exposed counties, such as some in the Midwest, are winners: they gain migrants, firm entry and, because more varieties raise utility, labor supply per worker.

  • Capital and housing: capital is rented at a fixed world real rate and housing is supplied locally as in Suarez Serrato and Zidar (2016). The output loss is 0.64 percent, because flood risk also lowers the capital-labor ratio (by 0.37 percent).

  • Heterogeneous firms: a Melitz (2003) version with fixed operating costs is calibrated to an exit rate of 0.08 and a 0.8 percent flood-induced rise in exits. The output loss is 0.51 percent, slightly smaller, because exiting firms are small.

Across extensions the losses are similar or somewhat larger, so the baseline forces dominate.

Counterfactual 3: Future Flood Risk, 2020-2050

rmr_mrm​ is scaled by First Street Foundation's county projections, under which the share of properties at risk rises by 4.5 percent on average. Output falls by 0.135 percent; employment falls by 0.057 percent, entry by 0.053 percent, exits by 0.037 percent and welfare by 0.055 percent. This is comparable to the 0.11 percent global GDP loss from sea-level rise in Desmet et al. (2021). The decomposition is direct damage 0.013, labor relocation 0.038, labor supply 0.058 and variety 0.026 percent, so only about 10 percent comes from direct damages. The projected risk increase is more positively correlated with regional productivity, so outflows from productive regions amplify the loss.

Takeaways

Expected flood risk, not realized floods, drives long-run firm entry and employment. Most of the output cost of flood risk comes from endogenous labor supply and fewer varieties, and only a minority from direct damages, so damage-based accounting understates the cost of climate hazards. Migration redistributes activity across counties but adds little to the aggregate loss. One caveat is that FEMA zone maps proxy perceived risk, so the paper cannot fully separate perceived from actual risk, or risk from mandatory flood insurance.
