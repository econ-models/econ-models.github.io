Reference: Duflo et al. (2018)

Model Setting

The paper asks whether regulatory discretion helps or hurts the enforcement of pollution standards. The setting is Gujarat, India, where the Gujarat Pollution Control Board (GPCB) regulates about 20,000 industrial plants under strict emission limits. Penalties are severe when applied (in 2008 the GPCB ordered 9% of sample plants to close, at least temporarily, sometimes by cutting off water and electricity), but the chance of being caught is low: the regulator has a limited inspection budget and chooses which plants to inspect, so half of plants are inspected less often than the prescribed rate while similar plants are inspected many times more. Discretion would hurt if plants bribe to avoid inspections or the regulator shirks; it would help if the regulator uses local information to target the dirtiest plants.

The paper combines a two-year field experiment on 960 plants with a structural model. The experimental treatment bundled two changes: more inspection resources, and the removal of discretion over the extra inspections, which were assigned at random. Because the treatment changes both at once, the model is needed to separate the value of resources from the value of discretion. The model has two stages. In the targeting stage, a benevolent regulator chooses an inspection rule, based on plant observables and a noisy signal of pollution, to maximize abatement subject to an inspection budget; plants decide whether to run their abatement equipment given the inspection threat. In the penalty stage, after an initial inspection, a regulatory machine follows fixed rules for followup and punishment, and plants solve a single-agent dynamic problem of when to comply. The penalty stage delivers the value to a plant of being inspected at a given pollution level, which drives abatement incentives in the targeting stage. The model abstracts from corruption in the assignment of inspections (bribes during inspections are allowed).

Experimental Design and Data

From a population of 3455 red-category (high pollution potential) small and medium plants in Ahmedabad, Surat and Valsad, the authors drew 960 plants: all 473 audit-eligible plants in Ahmedabad and Surat and 488 randomly chosen audit-ineligible plants. Between August 2009 and May 2011, the 481 treatment plants were assigned at least one annual initial inspection and up to four per year: one in the first quarter, then a new inspection each quarter with probability 0.66. Each morning inspection teams received a random list of treatment plants. Treatment and control inspection reports entered the same database without a flag, were analyzed by the same labs, and were followed up by the same officials. The regulator kept discretion over its existing inspection budget in both arms. Data come from an independent end-line survey (April to July 2011) of pollution and abatement costs, and from 9624 GPCB documents that the authors code into actions of the regulator (inspect, warn, punish, accept) and the plant (comply, ignore), linked into chains of interactions. In these chains 87% end after a single inspection, with the regulator accepting in the third round.

Experimental Evidence

The treatment was implemented: control plants were inspected 1.40 times per year, and treatment plants an additional 1.71 times (3.11 in total), with initial inspections up by 1.50 per year. Treatment plants were 0.21 more likely to have a pollution reading collected (control base 0.38), 0.22 more likely to be found above a standard, and received more citations (0.20) and closure warnings (0.077). But costly penalties (closure directions, equipment mandates, utility disconnections) did not rise significantly. The treatment reduced end-line pollution by 0.105 standard deviations (standard error 0.084), insignificant, and raised compliance by 3.7 percentage points (standard error 2.1) on a base of 61%, with mass shifting from just above to just below the standard. Maintenance spending rose by 838 dollars (standard error 499).

Three facts point to status quo targeting. First, the treatment cut the share of plants inspected below the prescribed rate from 50% to 13% but did not raise the number of plants with five or more inspections. Second, it did not find more extreme violators: 35 (10) treatment plants had a reading above five (ten) times the standard against 33 (12) in the control. Third, control-group end-line readings, which the regulator never saw, predict future inspections conditional on observables and past readings, so the regulator has its own pollution signals. Random inspections mostly found modest violators whom the regulator would not have punished anyway.

Identification and Sensitivity

The experiment is the key source of identification. Using the Andrews, Gentzkow and Shapiro (2017) sensitivity measure, the targeting parameters are most sensitive to the products of treatment with inspections and inspections squared: if treated plants' mean inspections rose from 2.93 to 3.93 per year, λ^2\hat{\lambda}_2λ^2​ would rise from 10.06 to 26.23 and λ^1\hat{\lambda}_1λ^1​ fall from −0.22-0.22−0.22 to −0.58-0.58−0.58 (if treatment raised inspections a lot, the status quo rule must have been steep). Information σ12/(σ12+σ22)\sigma_1^2/(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)σ12​/(σ12​+σ22​) depends on the dispersion of inspections and their covariance with pollution: a one standard deviation rise in mean inspections squared (7.53 to 17.12) would raise it by about 0.31 from a base of 0.02. Abatement efficacy ϕ^2\hat{\phi}_2ϕ^​2​ depends on the pollution residual in the treatment group (one standard deviation more treatment pollution moves it from −0.71-0.71−0.71 to −0.51-0.51−0.51) and on costs (a 100 dollar rise in mean maintenance cost among runners moves it to −0.53-0.53−0.53). These sensitivities refer to the unconstrained estimates. Moving σc\sigma_cσc​ from 0.25 to 1.00 moves ϕ^2\hat{\phi}_2ϕ^​2​ from −0.60-0.60−0.60 to −1.07-1.07−1.07, both within one standard deviation of the baseline.

Penalty Stage Estimates

The machine is much more likely to punish after high readings (punish coefficient 1.15 for 5+ times the standard) and less likely to warn or punish after prior warnings or plant compliance. In the penalty estimates (in thousands of dollars, 1474 plant moves), a constant punishment costs a plant τ^0=53.54\hat{\tau}_0=53.54τ^0​=53.54 (standard error 24.68). Letting the penalty vary with pollution gives τ^1=39.57\hat{\tau}_1=39.57τ^1​=39.57, τ^2=54.11\hat{\tau}_2=54.11τ^2​=54.11 and τ^3=41.51\hat{\tau}_3=41.51τ^3​=41.51, and flatness cannot be rejected. When inspections are also costly, the punishment cost falls to τ^0=28.12\hat{\tau}_0=28.12τ^0​=28.12 (standard error 20.88) with a per-inspection cost ν^0=9.67\hat{\nu}_0=9.67ν^0​=9.67 (3.07). With pollution-varying inspection costs, τ^0=36.71\hat{\tau}_0=36.71τ^0​=36.71 and ν^1,ν^2,ν^3=10.93,9.72,5.83\hat{\nu}_1,\hat{\nu}_2,\hat{\nu}_3=10.93,9.72,5.83ν^1​,ν^2​,ν^3​=10.93,9.72,5.83, so inspections cost one-third or less of punishment. The shock scale σ^a\hat{\sigma}_aσ^a​ is between 4.83 and 5.88 across specifications. A punishment of about 54,000 dollars is more than three times the mean equipment cost, and it matches lost profits from a median 24.5-day closure if variable margins are 20–30%. At the first plant move (t=2t=2t=2), the value of regulation ranges from −1160-1160−1160 dollars with no reading to −6240-6240−6240 dollars for a reading above five times the standard. Averaged over the first-inspection pollution distribution it is −2131-2131−2131 dollars, 40% from penalties and 60% from abatement capital. Penalties are costly but rare.

Targeting Stage Estimates

In the constrained model, treatment raises the targeting index by 0.095 (about two extra inspections per year at the margin), and running equipment lowers log pollution by ϕ^2=−1.902\hat{\phi}_2=-1.902ϕ^​2​=−1.902 (0.160), an 85% reduction, in line with engineering efficacies of air pollution control devices. The regulator sees little: σ^1=0.069\hat{\sigma}_1=0.069σ^1​=0.069 (0.003) against σ^2=1.033\hat{\sigma}_2=1.033σ^2​=1.033 (0.047), so less than 1% of pollution variance is observable. Yet it targets aggressively: λ^2=33.02\hat{\lambda}_2=33.02λ^2​=33.02 and λ^1=−0.395\hat{\lambda}_1=-0.395λ^1​=−0.395. An audit-eligible control plant in Ahmedabad expects 0.56 inspections at the 5th percentile of the observed shock and 3.48 at the 95th; half of plants get fewer than one inspection a year while the plants expected to be dirtiest may get ten. The mean log maintenance cost is μ^c=2.388\hat{\mu}_c=2.388μ^​c​=2.388. Without the optimality constraints, targeting is somewhat flatter (λ^2=10.06\hat{\lambda}_2=10.06λ^2​=10.06, λ^1=−0.220\hat{\lambda}_1=-0.220λ^1​=−0.220), the observed share is larger (σ^1=0.111\hat{\sigma}_1=0.111σ^1​=0.111, σ^2=0.864\hat{\sigma}_2=0.864σ^2​=0.864), and abatement is less effective (ϕ^2=−0.711\hat{\phi}_2=-0.711ϕ^​2​=−0.711, standard error 0.308). Very aggressive targeting is optimal only when abatement is large. The distance metric test rejects optimality (χ22=16.10\chi_2^2=16.10χ22​=16.10, p-value 0.0003), but the constrained and unconstrained rules lie nearly on top of each other except at the highest signals. The model fits the inspection distributions by arm and the pollution distribution, and it reproduces the experimental shift of mass from just above to just below the standard.

Counterfactuals: The Value of Discretion

Holding the penalty stage fixed (a medium-run exercise), the paper solves the regulator's problem for different budgets Iˉ\bar{I}Iˉ under four regimes: (1) a uniform rule giving every plant the same inspections (no information); (2) optimal discretionary targeting on the observed shock u1ju_{1j}u1j​; (3) discretionary targeting with full information, σ2=0\sigma_2=0σ2​=0, feasible with continuous emissions monitoring systems (CEMS); (4) a hybrid like the experiment, with the first 1.47 inspections (control mean) allocated with discretion and extra ones uniformly. The results are as follows.

(i) A uniform rule does very poorly at low budgets. At about 1.5 inspections per plant, mean abatement is only 0.06, since few plants have trivial maintenance costs and thin inspections leave preemptive abatement unprofitable.

(ii) Discretion triples abatement at the status quo budget (0.17 against 0.06); a uniform rule would need about 2.2 inspections per plant to match it. The value of discretion is largest at tight budgets.

(iii) The experimental hybrid adds about 1.5 random inspections and raises abatement by 0.14 standard deviations, because plants not targeted by the regulator get too few random inspections to abate. Allocating the same extra inspections with discretion would have raised abatement about 15% more (0.16), an arc elasticity of pollution with respect to discretionary inspections of −0.27-0.27−0.27.

(iv) Better information raises abatement by 30% on average across budgets, with the gain increasing in the budget. At the control budget it is worth about as much as a one-third larger budget allocated with discretion. CEMS for particulate matter cost about 1800 dollars per plant-year, against 70 dollars for one in-person inspection with sampling.

The overall lesson is that removing discretion would undercut enforcement even with weak institutions, extra resources alone help modestly, and poor information is a binding constraint.
