Model Setting

The paper studies how incomplete regulation of large firms interacts with business groups (conglomerates). The setting is China's Top 1,000 Energy Conservation Program, announced by the National Development and Reform Commission in April 2006 as part of the 11th Five-Year Plan (11FYP), whose national goal was to cut energy intensity by 20 percent between 2006 and 2011. The program covered the 1,008 industrial firms in nine energy-intensive industries whose 2004 energy consumption exceeded 180,000 tons of coal equivalent (tce); together they used 670 million tce in 2004, 47 percent of industrial and 33 percent of total Chinese energy use. Because selection was based on retrospective 2004 energy use and the list never changed, participation could not be manipulated. Although the stated goals were energy efficiency and a saving of 100 million tce by 2011, in practice local officials (whose promotion was tied to meeting provincial targets) enforced firm-level energy use quotas based on historical energy use and expected industry growth, checked by annual third-party audits. In 2012 the program was expanded into the Top 10,000 Energy Conservation and Carbon Reduction Program, covering 16,078 firms with 2010 energy use above 10,000 tce; Top 10,000 firms that were not in the Top 1,000 were unregulated during the 11FYP and serve as controls.

Data combine firm energy use from the China Environmental Statistics Database (CESD, 2001-2010), output and characteristics from the Annual Survey of Industrial Firms (ASIF), tax survey data for robustness, and shareholder records from China's Administrative Registration Data (CARD), used to build ownership networks (wholly owned subsidiaries, partly owned firms, shareholders, and firms owned by the shareholders, with at least 25 percent ownership at each of up to two levels). Top 1,000 firms have 2,466 related firms in the same four-digit industry, 2.45 per regulated firm, and a related firm is on average 19.3 percent as large as its regulated counterpart. Within conglomerates size falls almost geometrically with rank: the second-largest firm is 29 percent as large as the largest, and conglomerates with larger leading firms have more firms.

The paper proceeds in three steps: difference-in-differences (DiD) designs for the effects on regulated firms, on related firms, and on unrelated firms in more exposed industries; an industry equilibrium model of conglomerate production with Melitz (2003) monopolistic competition, in which the regulation is a quota on the energy use of the largest affiliate; and policy analysis that computes aggregate energy savings, the government willingness to pay that rationalizes the program, and alternative policies such as conglomerate-level regulation and energy taxes.

Counterfactual Policies

  • Coverage: lowering or raising ϕ~\tilde{\phi}ϕ~​ so the program covers 50 or 60 percent of industry energy (versus 56) moves along the same price-energy trade-off, so adding firms (as in the Top 10,000) does not improve the trade-off.

  • Stringency: varying 1−ξ1-\xi1−ξ from 5 to 30 percent raises both price increases and energy savings, with a slightly worse trade-off (GWTP of 167 dollars at 1−ξ=301-\xi=301−ξ=30 percent). Since tightening ξ\xiξ and lowering ϕ~\tilde{\phi}ϕ~​ have similar welfare effects, a government with limited audit capacity may prefer stricter limits on fewer firms.

  • Conglomerate-level regulation: capping the energy use of all affiliates at the level implied by the Top 1,000 program avoids distorting the within-conglomerate allocation, lowers the shadow cost, and achieves a 4.36 percent energy reduction (versus 3.97) for the same price increase, 10 percent more savings (about 5 million tce), justified for a GWTP of at least 154 dollars. It uses public ownership data and requires monitoring fewer than 20 percent as many firms as the Top 10,000 program.

  • Energy taxes: a size-dependent energy tax on all affiliates of conglomerates with ϕ>ϕ~\phi>\tilde{\phi}ϕ>ϕ~​ performs almost exactly like conglomerate-level regulation, and a universal energy tax (sϕ~=100s_{\tilde{\phi}}=100sϕ~​​=100 percent instead of 86) is only slightly better (GWTP of 153 dollars). These tax calculations do not rebate revenue and assume homogeneous abatement costs.
