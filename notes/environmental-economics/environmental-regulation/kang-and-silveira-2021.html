Reference: Kang and Silveira (2021)

Model Setting

The paper quantifies the benefits of regulatory discretion in the enforcement of water pollution rules in California. Penalties for observably similar violations differ across dischargers (disparities in punishment), and two stories can explain this: regulators may differ in their own preferences (how much they care about pollution, how costly it is for them to punish), or they may use their expertise to tailor penalties to dischargers whose compliance costs differ. The paper builds a principal-agent model in the spirit of Becker (1968) and Mookherjee and Png (1994) in which a regulator sets a penalty schedule for each facility, and the facility, privately informed about its compliance cost, chooses an unobserved negligence level that stochastically generates violations. The model is nonparametrically identified by combining the facility first-order condition with an exogenous increase in enforcement stringency in 2006 (following d'Haultfoeuille and Fevrier 2020) and the regulator first-order condition (following Luo, Perrigne and Vuong 2018). The estimates show that most of the heterogeneity in penalties comes from heterogeneity in compliance costs, not in regulator preferences, and that removing discretion (a one-size-fits-all schedule or a linear schedule) raises penalties and enforcement costs while increasing violations by facilities whose violations the regulator views as most harmful.

Institutional Setting and Data

Discharges into surface waters are regulated through NPDES permits under the Clean Water Act and California's Porter-Cologne Act. Permittees submit self-monitoring reports; 95% of recorded violations in 2000-2014 were self-reported. Enforcement is carried out by nine Regional Water Quality Control Boards (seven part-time members appointed by the governor, relying on technical staff), overseen by the State Water Resources Control Board. The enforcement policy gives a penalty methodology (initial amount based on extent, severity and sensitivity of the receiving water, then adjustments for conduct, ability to pay, economic benefit, enforcement costs and environmental justice) but leaves wide room for discretion; the board's final penalty differs from the staff assessment in 24% of actions. Serious violations (exceeding limits for a group I pollutant by 40% or a group II pollutant by 20%) and chronic nonserious violations (four or more in six months) are subject to a mandatory minimum penalty (MMP) of 3,000 dollars per violation. Enforcement resources differ a lot across boards (from 109 to 539 permits per enforcement staff member).

The source of identifying variation is the 2006 institutional changes: the launch of the CIWQS electronic tracking and reporting system (July 2005) and the creation of a statewide Office of Enforcement (July 2006). Both lowered the administrative burden of enforcement. After the changes, average MMP violations per facility-quarter fell from 1.39 (2002-2005) to 1.10 (2009-2014), and the average penalty per violation within four years rose by about 1,300 dollars (95%), driven by the probability of a penalty (0.42 before, roughly doubling), not by the amount conditional on a penalty (3,280 versus 3,211 dollars). The sample is 228 domestic wastewater treatment facilities, responsible for 73% of effluent violations statewide. Descriptively, penalties conditional on violation attributes vary with facility and local attributes (major facilities, high-income counties and counties approving the 2006 water bond Proposition 84 are penalized more), the per-violation penalty increases with concurrent violations in the quarter (a concurrent violation raises the penalty probability by 9.3 percentage points), and past violations do not matter, which motivates a static, nonlinear penalty schedule.
