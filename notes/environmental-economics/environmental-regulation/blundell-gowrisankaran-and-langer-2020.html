Reference: Blundell, Gowrisankaran and Langer (2020)

Model Setting

The paper quantifies the value of dynamic enforcement, in which regulatory actions depend on a plant's history of past violations and investments, in the enforcement of the US Clean Air Act Amendments (CAAA). The EPA designates repeat or egregious offenders as high priority violators (HPV) and subjects them to more inspections and higher fines. Such escalation mechanisms increase marginal deterrence for repeat violations while avoiding over-fining plants before they have a chance to fix one-time violations, which is valuable when fines are costly to the regulator, when remediation is slow and stochastic, when inspections give imperfect information, and when the regulator cannot contract on heterogeneous plant costs. The authors build a discrete-time dynamic game between a plant and a regulator. The regulator's inspection, violation, fine and status-transition behavior is captured by estimated conditional choice probabilities (CCPs) rather than by an estimated regulator utility function, and plants choose when to invest in pollution abatement. The plant cost parameters are allowed to differ across plants through a nonparametric random coefficients distribution on a fixed grid, estimated by a convex GMM estimator in the spirit of Fox et al. (2011) and Nevo, Turner and Williams (2016). The data are a quarterly panel from EPA's ECHO enforcement database, 2007:I-2013:III, with 2,355,908 plant/quarter observations on 107,705 plants in the seven most polluting NAICS sectors, merged with NEI emissions, AP3 county marginal damages and NAAQS non-attainment status. Counterfactuals that change the state-contingent fine policy show that removing dynamic enforcement would raise pollution damages by 164 percent holding fines constant, or raise fines by 519 percent holding damages constant.

Enforcement Institutions and Data Facts

Enforcement is carried out largely by ten EPA regions and by state agencies, so regions and states differ in budgets and enforcement preferences. All plants are inspected regularly; an inspection or a self-report may uncover a violation, moving the plant into regular violator status, where it faces more inspections and possible fines, and it returns to compliance only once all violations are resolved. Fines combine a gravity component (actual or potential harm, toxicity, sensitivity of the receiving environment, duration, history of noncompliance, ability to pay) and the economic benefit of noncompliance; political pressure, bankruptcy laws and caps limit the EPA's ability to levy large penalties. HPV status, assigned through ten general and five matrix criteria (some explicitly dynamic, such as "chronic or recalcitrant violator"), triggers intense oversight, higher fines and deadlines, and can be exited only after all violations are resolved. Compliance is high: 95.6 percent of plant/quarters are in compliance and 88.4 percent of plants are never out of compliance, while only 4.2 percent ever enter HPV status. Investment, inferred from ECHO resolution codes (94 percent of investments), PSD permits and HPV exits, occurs in 4.9 percent of regular-violator quarters and 17.5 percent of HPV quarters. Investment last quarter raises the probability of returning to compliance by 38 percentage points; an HPV plant that did not invest last quarter stays HPV with certainty, while one that did has a 49 percent chance of moving to compliance, 26 percent of moving to regular violator status and 25 percent of staying HPV. Investments made while in compliance raise subsequent violation rates, so they are treated as economic rather than prophylactic and are not counted. About 30 percent of investments are followed by another within six quarters, versus about 2.3 percent under i.i.d. investment, which motivates random coefficients.

In a two-parameter homogeneous model with costly investment and fines only and no shocks, a violator invests when the investment cost relative to the fine coefficient is below the expected reduction in discounted future fines:

so the ratio is identified by the lowest expected fine reduction at which plants invest, and adding logit shocks identifies the scale from how steeply the investment probability rises with that reduction. With four regulatory cost dimensions, identification requires independent variation in how investment shifts expected future inspections, violations, fines and HPV status; some comes from Ω~2\tilde\Omega_2Ω~2​, but cross-region, industry and gravity variation in Ω~1\tilde\Omega_1Ω~1​ is especially helpful. Counterfactuals

Counterfactuals keep the state-contingent inspection policy, violations and transitions fixed (by Assumption 1), change only the fine policy (and the HPV cost), re-solve each type's dynamic problem, and report long-run means weighted by the observed distribution of fixed states. Because regulator preferences are not recovered, these are plant best responses to alternative policies, not equilibria of a new game.
