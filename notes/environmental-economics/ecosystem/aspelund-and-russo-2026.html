Model Setting

The paper studies additionality as a problem of asymmetric information in markets for environmental services. A conservation contract is socially valuable only to the extent that it changes behavior, but landowners who would have conserved anyway tend to have low costs of accepting a contract and therefore win procurement mechanisms that screen on cost alone. The setting is the USDA's Conservation Reserve Program (CRP), one of the largest payments for ecosystem services (PES) schemes in the world, which pays landowners 1.6 to 1.8 billion dollars per year to retire cropland and undertake conservation actions under (typically ten-year) contracts. Contracts are allocated in a discriminatory, asymmetric scoring auction. The authors link confidential administrative data on all bids in the seven general-enrollment auctions of 2009-2021 to field-level land use from the 30-m Cropland Data Layer (2009-2020) and administrative Form 578 data (2013-2019), with aerial imagery to verify compliance. The paper has three parts: a stylized framework in the spirit of Einav, Finkelstein and Cullen (2010) showing that the cost distribution and an additionality function are sufficient statistics for welfare; reduced-form evidence that only about one quarter of marginal winners are additional and that additionality is positively correlated with bids (adverse selection); and a structural model of multidimensional bidding and additionality used to evaluate welfare under alternative auctions and scoring rules.

Auction Rules

Eligible landowners (who meet erosion standards, lie in a conservation priority area, and cropped at least four years in a five- to ten-year window before the auction, or are reenrolling) bid for heterogeneous contracts that "top up" the base action of land retirement with actions such as specific grass mixes, trees, or pollinator and rare habitats. A known scoring rule (in USDA practice, the Environmental Benefits Index) awards points for the environmental sensitivity of the land (erodibility, habitat importance, water and air quality, carbon), for the chosen contract, and for a low bid rental rate in dollars per acre per year, subject to a bid cap based on county rental rates and soil productivity. Congress fixes aggregate acreage in the Farm Bill, which determines the winning-score threshold; all bids scoring above it win. Because acreage and competitors are uncertain, bidders face uncertainty about the threshold. Crucially, the scoring rule is built under the assumption that land would be cropped absent the program, i.e. full additionality. Early exit or noncompliance is penalized by refunding all payments plus 25 percent. The average auction has 36,772 bidders; the average bidder offers 84.1 acres (33 percent of her land) at 83 dollars per acre-year, 70 percent are recontracting, and 81 percent win.

Extensions

  • Status quo format: the preferred scoring rule in the existing discriminatory auction, with beliefs G(S)G(S)G(S) and best responses iterated to a fixed point, gives 236 million without raising USDA spending (1,373 million). Switching to VCG alone adds only 20 million; changing the scoring rule adds over five times as much.

  • Limited-information rule: a rule built only from pre-2016 auctions, using cross-tract cropping heterogeneity and cross-auction RD coefficients in administrative data, gives 217 million in the 2016 holdout auction versus 91 million under the status quo in that year.

  • Spending: across auctions government spending exceeds the environmental value procured because of adverse selection, since the marginal landowner is more additional than inframarginal ones.

  • Welfare weights: the status quo is preferred to the best counterfactual only if a dollar to landowners is worth more than three times a dollar spent on conservation.
