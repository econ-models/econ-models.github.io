Model Setting

The paper builds a spatial and dynamic model of efficient land allocation in the Brazilian Amazon biome. Each piece of land can be used for cattle ranching (about 85% of deforested, non-abandoned land is pasture, so agriculture is identified with cattle) or kept as forest. Deforestation releases the carbon stored in primary forest, while abandoned pasture regrows as secondary forest and slowly absorbs carbon. A fictitious social planner, who internalizes the emissions externality, trades off cattle revenue against the value of carbon at an exogenous emissions price. The paper does not model decentralized landowners: since monitoring and enforcement cut deforestation by more than 80% between 2004 and 2012, current deforestation is read as reflecting government valuations, and edge effects and fires make conservation a decision at a scale far above private holdings.

Three ingredients distinguish the analysis from static or discrete-choice models of deforestation (e.g. Souza-Rodrigues (2019), Araujo, Costa and Sant'Anna (2025)): (1) explicit carbon accumulation dynamics in regrowing forests, so that reforestation, not only avoided deforestation, is a choice and transition paths over 15 to 30 years can be computed; (2) large cross-sectional heterogeneity in cattle productivity and carbon density, measured from census and satellite data on 1,043 sites (about 67.5 km by 67.5 km) or 78 coarse sites (about 270 km by 270 km); (3) uncertainty on two fronts: stochastic cattle prices following a Markov chain, and uncertain site productivities, handled with ambiguity aversion toward a baseline Bayesian posterior. The paper first infers a “business-as-usual” shadow price of emissions that rationalizes 1995–2008 deforestation, and then studies how international transfers of bbb dollars per net ton of CO2_22​e captured change land use, carbon and Brazil's welfare.

Solution Method

Deterministic, ambiguity-neutral case. With the cattle price fixed at its stationary mean and productivities at their baseline means, the planner's problem over 1,043 sites is solved as one large static optimization over all trajectories for 200 years, discretized in annual steps:

Xt+1i=Xti−γiUti−α[Xti−γi(zˉi−Zti)],Zt+1i=Zti+Uti−Vti(9.5.7.17)X_{t+1}^i=X_t^i-\gamma^iU_t^i-\alpha\left[X_t^i-\gamma^i\left(\bar{z}^i-Z_t^i\right)\right],\qquad Z_{t+1}^i=Z_t^i+U_t^i-V_t^i \tag{9.5.7.17}Xt+1i​=Xti​−γiUti​−α[Xti​−γi(zˉi−Zti​)],Zt+1i​=Zti​+Uti​−Vti​(9.5.7.17)

Stochastic prices. With price randomness, recursive HJB methods are infeasible given the heterogeneity and state constraints, so the paper uses model predictive control (MPC) on the 78-site grid. From each date, the future is split into an uncertainty horizon of τ\tauτ periods, over which controls are measurable functions of realized prices (the scenario tree grows geometrically in τ\tauτ), and the remaining periods, in which the price is frozen at its date-τ\tauτ value. The first-period decision is implemented, the state is updated, and the problem is re-solved. Comparing V(τ)−V(τ−1)V(\tau)-V(\tau-1)V(τ)−V(τ−1) gives τ∗=3\tau^*=3τ∗=3.

Key Results

  • Business as usual. With Pe=Pee=6.8P^e=P^{ee}=6.8Pe=Pee=6.8 and no transfers, agricultural area rises from 15% to about 20% of the biome over 30 years, a level that Lovejoy and Nobre (2018) and Flores et al. (2024) associate with possible tipping-point loss of the rainforest. Net emissions are about 12.1 Gt CO2_22​e over 15 years and 14.7 Gt over 30 years.

  • Transfers trigger large-scale restoration.
