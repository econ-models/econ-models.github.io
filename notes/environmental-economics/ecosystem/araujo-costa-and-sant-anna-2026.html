Model Setting

The paper estimates the carbon-efficient forest cover of the Brazilian Amazon, defined as the land use that would arise if farmers fully internalized the social cost of the carbon stored in each plot of forest. It builds a dynamic discrete choice model in which profit-maximizing agents choose every year whether each field is used for crops, pasture or forest, subject to conversion costs and with forward-looking option values. The model is estimated on a panel of land use for more than 5.7 billion 30-m pixels in each year 2008-2017 (MapBiomas), combined with potential yields (FAO GAEZ), crop prices at regional trade hubs (ESALQ), newly computed transportation costs to export ports, and a 30-m map of aboveground biomass (Zarin et al. 2016). The sample is pixels outside protected areas that were not deforested before 2000. Estimation follows Scott (2013): the CCP structure and one-period finite dependence deliver an Euler-type equation that is a linear regression with transition probabilities on the left-hand side, so the value function never has to be solved during estimation.

The setting motivates three features. First, deforestation is driven by pasture: between 2008 and 2017, 90% of the deforested area became pasture for extensive cattle ranching, and 7% of 2008 forest became pasture by 2017 versus only 1% that became cropland. Second, deforestation is not a one-way street: 13% of 2008 pasture had reverted to forest by 2017, so the model allows regeneration. Third, both private returns (yields, transport costs) and social damages (carbon density) vary sharply over space, so the conservation trade-off is local and policies that ignore this heterogeneity are inefficient. The key counterfactuals compare the business-as-usual (BAU) steady state with the efficient one under a social cost of carbon of 50 US dollars per ton of CO2_22​, and evaluate a carbon tax (equivalently, a carbon-based payment for ecosystem services) and excise taxes on cattle and crops.

Data and CCP Construction

MapBiomas classifies every 30-m pixel each year; uses are aggregated to crop, pasture, forest and other (other and protected areas are dropped). For each pair of years and each transition, a 0/1 matrix marks pixels making the transition and is averaged up to 1-km locations, at which suitability (ymcy_{mc}ymc​, ym,pasturey_{m,\text{pasture}}ym,pasture​), transport (zmcz_{mc}zmc​, dmd_mdm​), carbon (hmh_mhm​) and the shocks ξjmt\xi_{jmt}ξjmt​ are homogeneous. Many 1-km cells still have near-zero transition rates, which break the Hotz-Miller inversion, so CCPs are smoothed with a Gaussian filter (standard deviation 150 km) over latitude and longitude. Biomass is converted to potential CO2_22​ release (divide by 2 for carbon, multiply by 44/12), and all carbon is assumed released on clearing (slash and burn, fast decomposition). Transport costs are estimated by regressing ESALQ freight costs on quality-adjusted road distances by nonlinear least squares, as in Donaldson (2018), then adding waterways and computing least-cost bimodal routes to the nearest port with Dijkstra's algorithm; dmd_mdm​ is the quality-adjusted distance before monetization. The discount factor is fixed at ρ=0.9\rho=0.9ρ=0.9 (a 10% annual rate), since it is poorly identified.

Carbon and the Efficient Benchmark

The efficient land use sets farmers' perceived value of forest carbon equal to the social cost of carbon of 50 US dollars per ton of CO2_22​, i.e. a carbon tax of 47.26 US dollars on top of the 2.74 already perceived. A carbon tax on non-forest uses based on the potential carbon content of the land is equivalent in this model to a flow of payments for ecosystem services for carbon kept in the forest (or to tighter enforcement); it is implemented by raising the perceived carbon return αforest\alpha_{\text{forest}}αforest​. Counterfactual Results

Price elasticities. A 10% crop price increase gives a long-run cropland elasticity of 6.60, a pasture cross-elasticity of −0.25-0.25−0.25, a forest cover elasticity of −0.42-0.42−0.42 and a carbon-released elasticity of 0.13 (0.5 Gt of extra CO2_22​, 25 billion US dollars of damages). For cattle prices: forest −1.66-1.66−1.66, crop −0.44-0.44−0.44, pasture 1.43, carbon released 1.82, so cattle markets matter much more for forests. With myopic agents (ρ=0\rho=0ρ=0), the forest elasticities fall to −0.14-0.14−0.14 (crop price) and −0.47-0.47−0.47 (cattle price), confirming that static models understate long-run responses.

Efficient versus BAU. The efficient steady state preserves 99.7% of the carbon stock and 97% of the forest cover in the sample, against 51% of the carbon stock under BAU. The efficiency gap is 42 billion tons of CO2_22​ (95% CI 39-45) and 1,186,216 km2^22 of forest (about twice France), with 92% of the emissions gap coming from conversion to pasture. Closing it would require stopping the current deforestation pattern for about four decades. Locally the gap reaches 40,000 tons of CO2_22​ per km2^22 (2 million US dollars of damages per km2^22), mostly along waterways and roads in Para, where carbon density and agricultural profitability are both high; it is small at the low-carbon fringe (Mato Grosso, Tocantins) and in the remote far west. Regeneration matters little on the efficient path (primary forest is conserved) but a lot under BAU, where fields are exhausted and abandoned: ignoring it raises the gap by 42% after 15 years.

Carbon tax. The response is highly convex: a carbon tax of 2.5 US dollars per ton saves 25 billion tons of CO2_22​ and 526 thousand km2^22 (60% of the efficient savings), 5 dollars saves 34 billion tons (81%), and 10 dollars preserves 95% of the efficient carbon stock. Preserving carbon is cheap deep in the forest where carbon and transport costs are high, and land is the main input of extensive ranching, so a small rise in the perceived cost of clearing is a large cost shock. The 10 dollar figure is below the 18.50 dollars of Souza-Rodrigues (2019), reflecting post-PPCDAm enforcement and the dynamic model, but above the 5 dollars per ton paid by the Amazon Fund.

Cattle and crop taxes. An ad valorem tax on cattle ranching returns is a second-best instrument because it is not tied to carbon density. A 20% tax saves about 15.8 billion tons of CO2_22​; matching the 34 billion tons of a 5-dollar carbon tax needs a 60% cattle tax (50% matches its forest cover). Crop taxes barely change emissions, since crops are a small share of land use.

Welfare. Moving from BAU to the efficient path yields welfare gains of about 1.66 trillion US dollars: 2.02 trillion of avoided emission damages minus 356 billion of foregone private profits, more than five dollars of corrective gain per dollar of lost profit. Most gains are captured by carbon taxes below 15 dollars per ton. The welfare-maximizing cattle tax is 100%, which achieves 87% of the first-best gains (about 1.8 trillion of avoided damages, 273 billion of lost profits), because it also distorts land toward crops and taxes pasture even in low-carbon, high-productivity areas.

Extensions and Robustness

  • Tipping points. BAU deforests 57% of the sample; if protected areas stay intact and pre-2000 clearing does not regenerate, total Amazon deforestation reaches 31%, below the 40% dieback threshold. Other externalities (biodiversity, rainfall, tipping points) are omitted, so the gap is a lower bound.

  • Equilibrium effects. With a world beef supply elasticity of 1.43 and demand elasticity of −0.45-0.45−0.45, the efficient land use raises world beef prices by 4.16%, offsetting at most 5.9% of grazing area.

  • Trade. In a partial equilibrium model where Brazilian beef is sold to the EU and the rest of the world, the domestic price response to a change dtdtdt in the EU tariff is approximately:

  • Carbon accounting. If regenerated forest holds zero carbon for 30 years, the BAU-efficient gap rises to 58 billion tons, but a 10-dollar tax still saves 49 billion tons (40 in the baseline).   • Discount factor. With ρ=0.95\rho=0.95ρ=0.95 conversion costs are generally lower, the gap is 53 Gt, and a 10-dollar tax closes 92% of it.

  • Technology. Modeling crop returns with soy-maize double cropping gives a perceived carbon value of 2.86 reais per ton per year and an efficiency gap of 37 billion tons of CO2_22​.
