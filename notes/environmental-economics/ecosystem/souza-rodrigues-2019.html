Model Setting

The paper builds a framework to measure the cost effectiveness of conservation policies on private properties in the Brazilian Amazon: (1) payments for ecological services (PES), (2) taxes on agricultural land, and (3) the command-and-control “80 percent rule”, which requires landowners to keep 80% of their property as native forest (actual forest cover on private land is only about 44%). The key object is the demand for deforestation: how much private land farmers would keep in agriculture as the private value of forest relative to agriculture rises by ttt dollars per hectare. The demand is identified from regional variation in transportation costs to ports, which shift farmgate prices. Rescaling transportation costs ($/ton) by local yields (tons/ha) converts them into $/ha, so a change in transport costs is equivalent to a land use tax or payment. Transportation costs are instrumented with straight-line distances to the nearest port and state capital, and heterogeneous responses are captured with instrumental variable quantile regression (IVQR). Data: 2006 Agricultural Census (523 municipalities, four farm-size groups), the 2006 transportation network with freight rates, agroclimatic controls, and carbon stock maps.

Identification

TCmTC_mTCm​ is endogenous: (1) the proxy is measured with error (attenuation); (2) deforested regions demand better roads (reverse causality); (3) roads may target unobservably good farmland (omitted variables). The direction of the OLS bias is ambiguous, since early roads and rivers served rubber extraction in areas not necessarily suited to farming. The instruments are straight-line distances to the nearest port and nearest state capital. Ports, capitals and most municipal seats were established by the 19th or early 20th century, before the 1970s road expansion, so a virtual network of straight lines between them is plausibly exogenous to recent agricultural activity. Key Estimates

  • OLS coefficients on TCmTC_mTCm​ are small and insignificant (positive for small and medium farms); 2SLS coefficients are negative but imprecise. IVQR coefficients for medium and large farms are negative, mostly significant, larger in magnitude than QR, and vary across quantiles: lower-tail quantile curves are convex and upper-tail ones concave, so the mean effect hides large heterogeneity.

  • At the conditional median, raising transport costs by $10/ton barely changes the agricultural share of small farms (<5<5<5 ha), but lowers it by 1-1.5 p.p. on medium farms and by 2.5 p.p. on large farms (>500>500>500 ha). Small farms are mostly isolated and subsistence-oriented, so the strategy likely does not identify their demand, but they occupy only 1% of private land.

  • Land is very concentrated: large farms are 6% of farms but 52% of farmland, so the shape of total demand is driven by large farms. Policies targeting only smallholders cannot achieve much conservation, and PES cannot substantially reduce poverty and deforestation at the same time.

  • Holding post-2004 fines at pre-2004 levels shifts the demand curve up only slightly (agricultural share 56.2% to 59.3%): satellite-based monitoring was too recent to affect the 2006 stock.

Policy Analysis

Taxes and payments. Because both raise the value of forest relative to agriculture by ttt, land use taxes and payments have the same land use effects and the same economic cost; they differ only in who pays. The farmers' lost surplus from a tax ttt, with revenues rebated to them, is the area under the demand curve net of the rebate:

CS(t)=∫0tD(τ) dτ−t D(t)(9.5.1.11)CS(t)=\int_0^tD(\tau)\,d\tau-t\,D(t) \tag{9.5.1.11}CS(t)=∫0t​D(τ)dτ−tD(t)(9.5.1.11)

  • A tax of $42.5/ha per year cuts agricultural area from 43.2 million ha (56% of private land) to 15.5 million ha (20%), i.e. 80% forest cover, preserving about 27.7 million ha (about 5.5 years of global net forest loss). The lost surplus is about $479 million per year, with tax revenue of about $658 million (0.37% of the 2006 federal budget). Average gross revenue is only $120/ha, so such a tax drives many farmers out.

  • Equivalent payments of $42.5/ha yield the same forest cover and cost but require $2.61 billion per year in transfers (1.45% of the federal budget); perfect targeting would halve the transfers ($1.18 billion) but is unlikely under asymmetric information.

  • Farmers in the soybean region of the South Amazon (the “Arc of Deforestation”) respond little even to $100/ha taxes, while forests in the central and western Amazon are cheaper to preserve.

The 80 percent rule. A lower bound on its hidden cost uses municipality- and farm-size-specific taxes tm(s)t_m(s)tm​(s) that would bring each group's agricultural share down to 20%, Ym(s,tm(s))=0.2Y_m(s,t_m(s))=0.2Ym​(s,tm​(s))=0.2, and sums the lost surplus (trapezoids under each demand curve):

This is about $4 billion per year, so the uniform tax achieves the same forest cover at about 90% lower cost (versus 20-47% cost savings of cap-and-trade over standard caps). The rule is not cost effective because the most productive farms must give up the most profitable land. Using 2SLS instead of IVQR, the 80%-cover tax would be $65/ha with a cost of $786 million (64% higher), showing the importance of heterogeneity.

Carbon. Let ΔCm\Delta C_mΔCm​ be the difference in carbon stock between forested and deforested land in mmm (Baccini et al. 2012; about 80 tC/ha on average), assumed released immediately upon clearing. A carbon tax (or payment) of ccc dollars per ton of CO2_22​ per year is equivalent to a land use tax tm=c×4412×ΔCmt_m=c\times\frac{44}{12}\times\Delta C_mtm​=c×1244​×ΔCm​, which traces out a supply of avoided emissions:

A carbon price of only $1/tCO2_22​ per year would avoid about 4.17 billion tons of carbon (about 4.5 years of global land use emissions in 2002-11), far below the EU ETS price ($8.5/tCO2_22​ in December 2006). A tax at the social cost of carbon (about $18.5-21/tCO2_22​) would virtually eliminate agricultural land in the Amazon, given the low agricultural returns.

Limitations. Costs exclude monitoring and transaction costs (INPE's and IBAMA's budgets, $125 and $560 million, are comparable to the $658 million of tax revenue), and general equilibrium effects (the Amazon is 20% of Brazil's agricultural area) as well as responses of farm size and of occupation of unprotected public land are not modeled.
