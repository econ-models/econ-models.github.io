Reference: Farrokhi et al. (2025)

Model Setting

The paper studies deforestation in a dynamic world trading system: how trade barriers affect the extent, location and timing of forest loss. Four empirical patterns motivate the model (FAO data, 1990-2020): (1) global forest area fell by 7.1%, with about 90% of the loss in tropical countries and regrowth in Europe and China; (2) deforestation moves with agricultural land expansion; (3) countries with a revealed comparative advantage in agriculture in 1990 expanded agricultural land and lost more forest; (4) deforestation follows from a country's own population growth and that of its main trading partners. The model has a market for new land: a land-clearing sector uses labor to convert open-access forests into agricultural land, and forward-looking landowners buy new land and rent it to producers. Three mechanisms shape land use: (1) structural change determines the size of agriculture and the aggregate demand for land; (2) comparative advantage distributes the pressure on land across countries; (3) forward-looking land accumulation determines the timing of forest responses.

The paper first derives analytical results in a stylized steady state, then calibrates a quantitative version (40 countries/regions, multiple agricultural industries, manufacturing, services) and simulates the removal of agricultural tariffs, with and without UN population projections to 2100, including the carbon costs of deforestation.

  • λ\lambdaλ vs. γ\gammaγ: higher labor elasticities scale up the overall response, while a higher λ\lambdaλ lowers future clearing productivity and makes short-run responses relatively larger; the differences across 10-, 20- and 30-year horizons separate them.

  • ζi,F,ζi,O\zeta_{i,F},\zeta_{i,O}ζi,F​,ζi,O​: from initial land stocks being a steady state; ζi,F\zeta_{i,F}ζi,F​ correlates with measures of forest protection.

  • Validation: the fallow block is needed to match the land response to population; the model reproduces the (untargeted) effect of trading partners' population growth and correlates with observed yields.

Counterfactuals

  • Scenario 1: remove all tariffs on Brazilian agricultural exports (an 8.6 p.p. tariff cut). Brazil's forest share falls by 1 p.p. after 14 years, 2 p.p. after 42 years and 2.5 p.p. in the steady state; global forest area falls. Steady-state forest leakage (forest gain abroad per unit of forest lost in Brazil) is about 50%, and is larger in the short run because Brazil cannot reallocate workers to land clearing immediately.

  • Scenario 2: remove all agricultural tariffs globally. Global forest area rises by about 0.2 p.p. (12 million ha after 30 years, 20 million ha in the steady state), versus 291 million ha lost in 1990-2020. Structural change dominates the direct effect of lower tariffs. Behind the small aggregate change is large reallocation: Brazil, the US, Canada and Australia account for over three-quarters of the forest loss (1-3% of their forests), while forest expands in most tropical countries (e.g. Central Africa, Southeast Asia).

  • Mechanisms: with σ∈{1.5,3.0}\sigma\in\{1.5,3.0\}σ∈{1.5,3.0} (substitutes) Scenario 2 instead causes global forest loss, while Scenario 1 is qualitatively unchanged. Across countries, a stronger comparative advantage in agriculture predicts more forest loss (slope −0.777-0.777−0.777). Comparative and absolute advantage in agriculture are positively correlated, so global agricultural land falls by about 1% while land-weighted agricultural productivity rises by 8%, in line with Proposition 4.

  • Population growth (UN projections, 35% on average, over 300% in parts of Africa): large deforestation in Africa in the new baseline; global tariff removal then has a slightly negative effect on global forest, but shifts deforestation away from carbon-dense African forests.

  • Welfare: climate-adjusted welfare is C−ϕZC-\phi ZC−ϕZ (world real consumption minus the value of deforestation emissions, no sequestration from regrowth, 5% discount rate). Global tariff removal raises real consumption by 0.131%; with SCC = $200/tCO2_22​ the climate cost is 8.8% of 2010 US GDP and the net gain falls to 0.086% (about 30% of the gain eroded); with SCC = $350 it is 0.053%; with population growth the climate cost falls to 4.5% and the net gain is 0.159%. The net gain turns negative only if the SCC exceeds about $1,150. Brazil-only liberalization lowers global welfare (−0.090%-0.090\%−0.090% before climate costs). With σ=1.5\sigma=1.5σ=1.5 the model would wrongly predict climate costs of 37.5% of US GDP. Gains across countries rise with the deforestation the policy induces.

Extensions

  • Non-homothetic preferences (Comin et al. 2021) change little.

  • A tenfold increase in tariffs worldwide yields net welfare losses under all carbon-cost and population scenarios.

  • 30% sequestration from regrowth lowers climate costs. Without carbon heterogeneity across forests, about 55% sequestration would make climate costs zero; with heterogeneity, near-complete sequestration is needed, because relocation offsets the gain in area.

  • Dynamics matter: the first 5 years account for one-tenth of the present value of climate costs from global tariff removal, and the first 20 years for 78%.

  • Higher λ\lambdaλ for boreal and temperate countries reduces deforestation in Canada, the US and Australia but also limits regrowth elsewhere, leaving aggregates unchanged.
