Model Setting

The paper asks whether spatially targeted forest conservation survives in general equilibrium, or whether the deforestation it prevents simply relocates, a phenomenon called leakage. It studies two flagship Brazilian policies over 2003-2018: the Priority List (from 2008), which singled out the municipalities with the highest deforestation rates for intensified command-and-control enforcement (embargoes, monitoring priority, credit and registration conditions), and the continuous creation of Protected Areas (Conservation Units and Indigenous Territories), where clearing is banned and which typically cover high-forest, low-deforestation regions. Reduced-form evidence shows both bind locally: a synthetic difference-in-differences design finds that listing cut total natural-vegetation loss by 0.44 log points (36%) while pasture area fell, cropland rose, agricultural value added per hectare rose by roughly 15% and cattle per hectare of pasture by roughly 20%; a spatial regression discontinuity finds forest cover 13-15 percentage points higher just inside Protected Areas. Such designs cannot measure leakage, because they assume untreated regions are unaffected.

The authors build an open-economy quantitative spatial model of Brazil in which deforestation is an economic sector: clearing forest is a costly, decreasing-returns activity that endogenously supplies agricultural land, so each region has an upward-sloping supply curve of new land whose position reflects its deforestation productivity. The economy has 557 micro-regions indexed by rrr (with o,do,do,d for origin and destination) plus a rest of the world (RoW), K=3K=3K=3 agricultural goods (temporary crops, permanent crops, pasture-based cattle) produced with land and labor, and one non-agricultural good. Preferences are non-homothetic (PIGL), workers migrate across regions and select into sectors, goods are traded with iceberg costs, and land accumulates over time. Brazil is modeled as a sequence of static spatial equilibria linked by laws of motion for land and labor. The central theoretical message is that leakage depends on whether targeted deforestation is supply-driven (land is cheap to clear) or demand-driven (land is valuable once cleared), and Brazilian deforestation is predominantly supply-driven.

Theory of Leakage

A stripped-down economy organizes the results. Let εD\varepsilon^DεD be the price elasticity of aggregate demand for newly cleared land. In a frictionless benchmark (perfect substitutes across origins, free labor mobility) there is one national price of new land, so deforestation is a pure ZDZ^DZD phenomenon. L=(1−b)εS(1−b)εS+εD(9.5.6.14)\mathcal{L}=\frac{(1-b)\varepsilon^S}{(1-b)\varepsilon^S+\varepsilon^D} \tag{9.5.6.14}L=(1−b)εS+εD(1−b)εS​(9.5.6.14)

Leakage L\mathcal{L}L (increase in non-targeted clearing per unit directly removed) rises with εS\varepsilon^SεS, falls with εD\varepsilon^DεD and falls with bbb. The logic is tax incidence: banning the biggest (cheapest) suppliers leaves a steep residual supply curve, so the induced land-price rise chokes off demand (intensification, substitution toward land-light goods) instead of relocating clearing. Even as b→0b\to0b→0, leakage is capped at εS/(εS+εD)\varepsilon^S/(\varepsilon^S+\varepsilon^D)εS/(εS+εD). Key Results

  • Leakage is small. Over 2003-2018 the Priority List avoided 80,051 km2^22 gross, with 2,935 km2^22 leaking back: 77,116 km2^22 net and leakage of 3.7%. Protected Areas avoided 7,311 km2^22 gross, 467 km2^22 leaked, 6,844 km2^22 net, leakage 6.4%. Net avoided emissions are 4.1 and 0.34 Gt of CO2_22​; carbon leakage is 3.0% and 5.5%, lower because targeted regions are carbon-dense.

  • Range. Re-solving at the frontier δ=0.62\delta=0.62δ=0.62, leakage rises to 8.8% (Priority List) and 14.7% (Protected Areas), so the policies retain at least about 91% and 85% of their gains. Only far above the identified range does leakage grow (about 12% and 20% at δ=0.70\delta=0.70δ=0.70; 20% and 32% at δ=0.80\delta=0.80δ=0.80).

  • Geography. About 60% of the Priority List's general-equilibrium effect occurs inside listed regions (in-situ price responses); relocation to untargeted regions is about 1.5% of avoided deforestation, some 1,200 km2^22. Absorption is concentrated (the top decile of the 526 untargeted regions takes 45%) and is predicted by the receivers' ZrDZ^D_rZrD​ with an elasticity near one, while market access enters negatively: displaced clearing flows to the next-cheapest, remote clearers.

  • Targeting. Holding scope fixed, the actual Priority List and a highest-deforestation rule both leak 3.7%, a highest-ZDZ^DZD rule leaks 3.3%, and a highest-land-value (demand-driven) rule leaks 36% while avoiding almost nothing (732 km2^22 net, against 79,635 for the actual policy in the same exercise); the value-to-area ratio of that rule is 11.

  • Horizon. Leakage is a path: 0.7% in the first policy year rising to 3.7% by 2018, as predicted by the stock-flow logic of Remark 1.

Extensions

Open versus closed economy: switching off RoW gives 3.2% (Priority List) and 5.7% (Protected Areas), because world demand both raises εD\varepsilon^DεD and makes demand for Brazilian land less satiable, and these nearly cancel. Parameter sweep: 54 of 55 single-deviation re-inversions converge; apart from δ\deltaδ, no parameter moves Priority List leakage by more than 0.6 percentage points (largest mover α\alphaα), shutting internal migration leaves it at 3.7% (workers switch sectors locally instead), and the Priority List leaks less than Protected Areas in every converged cell. Swap counterfactual: reassigning all variation to the demand side cannot reproduce Brazil's deforestation dispersion. SUTVA bias: running the synthetic difference-in-differences estimator on model-generated panels, spillovers attenuate the estimate by 0.013 log points (about 3%), and the model analogue (−0.43-0.43−0.43) matches the actual estimate (−0.44-0.44−0.44). Structural transformation and welfare: by 2018 the Priority List raises the national agricultural employment share by 0.10 percentage points (about 96,000 workers) through intensification and leaves real income per worker essentially unchanged; the implied general-equilibrium cost is about −0.001-0.001−0.001 dollars per tonne of CO2_22​ (Priority List) and −0.004-0.004−0.004 (Protected Areas), far below social costs of carbon of roughly 50 to 190 dollars, though this excludes fiscal enforcement costs and masks distributional incidence.
