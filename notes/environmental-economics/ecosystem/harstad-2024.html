Reference: Harstad (2024)

Model Setting

The paper builds a deliberately simple dynamic game to show how international trade and the exploitation of a depletable natural resource interact, and how trade policy can be redesigned to reverse the negative interactions. The South (SSS) owns a resource stock (the leading example is a tropical rainforest), and the North (NNN) suffers a damage whenever the resource is exploited. Logging produces timber and converts forest into agricultural land (beef), and both products can be exported to NNN. The motivating episode is the 2019 EU–Mercosur agreement, concluded while deforestation in the Amazon was rising, and the 2020 proposal by France and the Netherlands to link tariff reductions to the implementation of sustainability provisions.

The paper delivers two sets of results. Under a standard free trade agreement (FTA), (1) trade causes exploitation, because export prices exceed domestic valuations; (2) exploitation causes trade, because an FTA is only socially valuable once little resource is left to lose; and (3) SSS exploits in order to obtain trade, so the parties end up trading and depleting even when the damage exceeds the gains from trade. Under a contingent trade agreement (CTA), in which tariffs are functions of the remaining stock and are renegotiation proof, all these results are reversed: SSS faces low tariffs while the stock is large and high tariffs if it shrinks, so conservation is rewarded with a larger share of the gains from trade. Combined with export subsidies by NNN, the CTA implements the first best; without subsidies, conservation is limited by the size of the gains from trade.

The key idea, borrowed from dynamic games with renegotiation, is that punishments must stay on the Pareto frontier: after exploitation the parties still trade, but the gains are reallocated toward the harmed party. This is nontrivial because the ex post frontier expands as the resource is depleted.

The CTA exploits this multiplicity. Export subsidies are generally prohibited by the WTO. Without them, the transfer to SSS is bounded by the gains from trade, which limits how much conservation can be bought.

Comparison with the FTA and Discussion

With a CTA, Propositions 1–5 are overturned: (1) the agreement leads to more conservation than autarky; (2) the agreement is more valuable when RTR_TRT​ is large; (3) SSS conserves to keep the most attractive agreement; (4) in equilibrium SSS conserves when gains from trade are large and RtR_tRt​ is small; (5) τS\tau_SτS​ strictly increases or τN\tau_NτN​ strictly decreases (or both) in RTR_TRT​ and in RtR_tRt​. The CTA yields strictly higher payoffs than the FTA whenever the first best requires conservation but free trade leads to exploitation, aˉ+bˉ−c−d<0<aˉ+bˉ−c\bar{a}+\bar{b}-c-d<0<\bar{a}+\bar{b}-caˉ+bˉ−c−d<0<aˉ+bˉ−c. The online Appendix shows that the equilibrium CTA attains what the parties would obtain if they could commit to a stock-contingent agreement, so it implements the first best with export subsidies and the second best otherwise; it also shows the CTA can be robust to unilateral requests to renege and that exhaustibility is the key assumption. The companion paper Harstad (2024, “Contingent Trade Agreements”) adds nonlinear utilities, noncooperative tariffs as the threat point, and multiple goods and countries, confirms that a CTA is always strictly better than an FTA, and finds in a calibration that a CTA offered by the EU and the US can end or limit deforestation. If the WTO permitted export subsidies for conservation purposes, more forest could be conserved.
