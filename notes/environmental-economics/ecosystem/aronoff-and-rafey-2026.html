Model Setting

The paper builds an empirical framework for valuing environmental offset markets and applies it to Florida wetlands. Under the Clean Water Act and the 1972 Florida Water Resources Act, regulators enforce a No Net Loss rule: a landowner may develop a protected wetland only if the loss is offset by an equal gain elsewhere in the same hydrological region. Since the Florida banking rules of 1994, the offsets are produced by wetland mitigation banks, long-lived firms that place private land under a permanent conservation easement, restore or create wetlands, and receive certified offset credits from the regulator over time as restoration is verified. Developers buy credits bilaterally (often through brokers) from banks located in the same service area, a market boundary that approximates a hydrological region. The regulator converts wetland attributes into credits with a uniform assessment method that captures ecological and hydrological functions, but it does not account for the flood protection that wetlands provide to nearby property.

The new data cover 1995-2018: an administrative ledger of all state offset transactions, contracts and release schedules for every bank, transaction prices from a private broker and public-records requests, 30-m NOAA C-CAP land cover maps for 1996, 2001, 2006, 2011 and 2016, public land ownership, and FEMA flood insurance claims, policies and flood hazard maps, all matched to 1,378 local watersheds (1,004 inside offset markets). Five facts motivate the model. Cumulative sales were about 1.1 billion dollars (2020 dollars), growing about 9.8% per year. Markets are concentrated: fewer than three banks trade in an average market, the median bank produces about 200 credits over its life while the median developer buys 1.1 credits, and the average bank owns 26.1% of its market's production potential. Banks take time to build: the regulator typically releases 15% of a bank's credits every 3 years, about 18.2 years for a full project, and the median bank holds 52% of its credits in reserve. Prices (about 88,000 dollars per credit) exceed observed bank costs (about 24,000 dollars per credit). Finally, trade relocates wetlands from densely developed, flood-exposed cores to peripheral watersheds near public conservation land.

The model has three blocks: (1) many small, myopic landowners decide each period whether to develop their wetland, which requires buying credits at the market price (offset demand); (2) a few large banks enter by paying a sunk cost, receive credits on a fixed release schedule, and sell them over time in a dynamic oligopoly with storage (offset supply); (3) flood damages in each watershed depend on wetlands lost to development and wetlands restored by banks. Gains from trade are measured relative to the pre-market benchmark of direct conservation, under which the wetlands that were developed would have been conserved and the banks would not have entered.

Identification: Offset Demand

Prices respond to banks' beliefs about ξht\xi_{ht}ξht​, so price is endogenous. Three sets of supply-side cost shifters serve as instruments. (1) Historical sunk capacity: average capacity of past entrants whose service areas contain hhh. Capacity is fixed at entry and cannot be revised, so conditional on information known at entry it is an excluded shifter of future costs (Berry and Compiani (2023)); slow release (about 18 years) keeps it relevant. The threat is that entrants had private information on future demand shocks; bankers report forecasting from public information. (2) Hausman (1996) instruments: prices and historical entrant capacity in other markets of the same water district. (3) Public wetland and conservation land in other watersheds of the same market, which shifts restoration costs through land availability and connectivity, with some time variation from the Florida Forever and Preservation 2000 buyback programs. All three lower prices as theory predicts.

The implied return on capital averages 6.1% (IQR 1.8-8.3%), similar to the 5.86% real return on US housing. Unconditional costs are much higher than realized ones, showing strong selection into banking.

  • Floods: controlling for historical claims, ζ^d=0.245\hat{\zeta}_d=0.245ζ^​d​=0.245 (about half the naive 0.428) and ζ^b=−0.093\hat{\zeta}_b=-0.093ζ^​b​=−0.093; 10 percentage points more storm-surge zone raises expected damages by 29.2%. Annual damage spillovers from developing wetlands average about 1,400 dollars per hectare and 25,200 dollars per hectare in storm-surge zones, more than an order of magnitude below what Taylor and Druckenmiller (2022) would imply for Florida.

Welfare: Flood Externalities

Development is irreversible, so the social cost of lost flood protection is a perpetuity of annual marginal damages implied by the flood damage function, discounted at a 5% real rate (federal guidelines), with 3% and 7% as alternatives. Writing MDhMD_hMDh​ for the annual marginal damage per credit in hhh (this formula is written in words in the paper), the externality per credit is Eh=∑t=0∞0.95t MDhE_h=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}0.95^t\,MD_hEh​=∑t=0∞​0.95tMDh​. The hydrological externality is extremely heterogeneous: about 6,600 dollars per credit in a first-tercile watershed (negligible relative to an 88,000 dollar price), but 792,000 dollars and 1.6 million dollars per credit at the 90th and 95th percentiles, above observed prices. Wetlands lost to offset trade during 1996-2016 would have delivered 1.7-1.9 billion dollars of flood protection; the baseline flood damage is 1,888 million dollars (1,133 million at 7% and 2,643 million at 3%), so market welfare relative to direct conservation is only 522 million dollars. Damages arise because flood protection is positively correlated with the opportunity cost of conservation and uncorrelated with where banks choose to locate.

Counterfactuals

Solving a new dynamic oligopoly equilibrium is infeasible, so counterfactuals are benchmarks that hold the set of incumbent banks fixed, keep the estimated trading policies where the caps do not bind, and recompute the myopic Cournot caps under the new demand. The main reform is a locally differentiated Pigouvian tax: developers in hhh pay a tax per credit τh\tau_hτh​ equal to the expected marginal damage of observed development in their watershed, and bank sales in market mmm are subsidized in proportion to average expected flood protection from banks there. The developer's decision rule becomes (an explicit restatement of the reform described in words)

The reform keeps No Net Loss and can be implemented by prices or quantities on either side.

  • Pigouvian tax: development falls from 141,606 to 120,098 acres, private gains fall from 2,410 to 2,065 million dollars, flood damage falls from 1,888 to 282 million (more than 80% of damages, or 1.6 billion dollars, avoided), and welfare rises from 522 to 1,783 million. Each dollar of forgone gains buys more than 4 dollars of flood protection, (1,888−282)/(2,410−2,065)=1606/345=4.66(1{,}888-282)/(2{,}410-2{,}065)=1606/345=4.66(1,888−282)/(2,410−2,065)=1606/345=4.66. Tax revenue is 185 million; returning it lump sum to banks cuts the producer surplus loss from 26% to about 1%.

  • Uniform tax: the welfare-maximizing Florida-wide tax is 97,000 dollars per credit (about the mean price). It cuts development to 75,169 acres and damages to 715 million, but private gains fall to 1,831 million and welfare is 1,117 million, about half the gain of the local design.

  • Market structure: with full passthrough avoided damages are 103% and welfare gains 96% of the benchmark; banks expand supply under the reform, lowering producer prices by 3.9% (consumer passthrough 78.8%). Under myopic Cournot prices fall 6.2% (passthrough 70.3%), under myopic collusion passthrough is 99.8% because of logit curvature. Banks capture 46% of gains if they collude versus 26-30% otherwise. Flood benefits equal 127-159% of the welfare gain and 2.7-4.6 times the forgone private gains, so compensating transfers could make the reform a Pareto improvement.

Extensions

Several caveats bound the results. Developers are myopic and act independently, so strategic delay and coordinated multi-parcel projects are ruled out. Counterfactuals hold entry fixed and do not let banks update beliefs over future states, so lower entry under weaker demand is not captured. Flood damages use insured claims only, ignoring uninsured losses, losses above policy limits and defensive investment. Welfare assumes lawful implementation of No Net Loss for nonflood wetland functions. Alternative designs include removing very high-risk watersheds from service areas or local permit denials. The framework applies to other offset markets where production differs from abatement, suppliers are concentrated, verification takes years, or some environmental dimensions are left out of trading rules.
