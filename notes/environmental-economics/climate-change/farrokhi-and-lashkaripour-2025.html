Model Setting

The paper asks how far trade policy can go in solving the free-riding problem in international climate agreements. Each government cares only about the climate damage borne by its own residents, so the unilaterally optimal carbon tax is far below the social cost of carbon. Two trade-policy remedies are evaluated: (1) noncooperative carbon border taxes, which tax the carbon content of foreign goods directly; and (2) Nordhaus's (2015) climate club, in which members raise carbon taxes to a common target and threaten nonmembers with trade penalties. The paper builds a multi-country, multi-industry general equilibrium trade model with a global carbon supply chain, derives closed-form formulas for unilaterally optimal carbon and border taxes using a dual decomposition technique, and uses these formulas to compute the policy frontier of both proposals with GTAP 2014 data (19 countries/regions, 17 nonenergy industries plus energy).

Production is a two-stage process: (1) each country's energy industry (k=0k=0k=0) uses labor and carbon reserves to extract energy, which is traded internationally; (2) final-good industries (k=1,…,Kk=1,\dots,Kk=1,…,K) combine labor and an energy bundle to produce goods, which are also traded. CO2_22​ is counted when energy is used by final-good producers and households. Markets are perfectly competitive, and no private agent internalizes the effect of its choices on global emissions.

Global Optimum and Free-Riding

A global planner chooses all instruments and lump-sum transfers to maximize ∑iϑiln⁡Wi\sum_i\vartheta_i\ln W_i∑i​ϑi​lnWi​. The globally optimal policy taxes carbon at the sum of all countries' disutilities and sets all border taxes to zero, since carbon taxes target emissions directly:

The free-riding problem is the gap τi∗=δ~i<τ⋆=∑nδ~n\tau_i^*=\tilde{\delta}_i<\tau^\star=\sum_n\tilde{\delta}_nτi∗​=δ~i​<τ⋆=∑n​δ~n​: if all others commit to τ⋆\tau^\starτ⋆, each country wants to lower its own tax to δ~i\tilde{\delta}_iδ~i​, triggering a race to the bottom. The paper evaluates two remedies:

  • Proposal 1 (carbon border taxes): every government adopts the unilaterally optimal carbon and border taxes of Proposition 2 noncooperatively. The resulting equilibrium is the Nash equilibrium of a one-shot policy game.

  • Proposal 2 (climate club): a set of core countries N(core)\mathbb{N}^{(core)}N(core) sets a carbon tax target τ(target)\tau^{(target)}τ(target). Members raise domestic carbon taxes to τ(target)\tau^{(target)}τ(target), keep free trade among members, and impose the unilaterally optimal trade taxes (t∗,x∗)(t^*,x^*)(t∗,x∗) of Proposition 2 as penalties on nonmembers. Nonmembers retaliate with their own optimal trade taxes against members but otherwise keep status quo policies.

A partition into members and nonmembers is a Nash equilibrium of the club game if no noncore country wants to switch sides and every core country is better off than in the status quo:

A higher target cuts more emissions per member but discourages participation, so emission reductions are inverted-U (Laffer-curve) shaped in τ(target)\tau^{(target)}τ(target). Since penalties are meant to deter rather than be used, the target is set at the maximal rate that still supports a club of all nations.

Quantitative Strategy

The counterfactual equilibrium under optimal policy jointly solves two mappings: (a) the equilibrium given taxes, RV=g(RT;B)\mathcal{R}_V=g(\mathcal{R}_T;\mathcal{B})RV​=g(RT​;B), solved in exact hat algebra relative to the 2014 status quo; and (b) the optimal tax formulas given the equilibrium, RT=f(RV;B)\mathcal{R}_T=f(\mathcal{R}_V;\mathcal{B})RT​=f(RV​;B). The algorithm iterates between the two. Required inputs B\mathcal{B}B are observed baseline shares (expenditure, employment, emissions, energy cost shares, carbon intensities), applied 2014 taxes, and parameters {δ~i,φi,ς,σk}\{\tilde{\delta}_i,\varphi_i,\varsigma,\sigma_k\}{δ~i​,φi​,ς,σk​}. For the club game, the optimal penalties come from the closed-form formulas, avoiding numerical optimization over 2N−N(core)2^{N-N^{(core)}}2N−N(core) partitions. Instead, an iterative procedure (similar to iterated elimination of dominated strategies) adds countries that gain from joining given the current club, until convergence. An outer loop raises τ(target)\tau^{(target)}τ(target) until universal membership fails.

Key Results

  • Optimal border taxes: absent climate concerns, the median optimal tariff-to-subsidy wedge (1+t∗)/(1+x∗)(1+t^*)/(1+x^*)(1+t∗)/(1+x∗) is 1.17 (10th-90th percentile 1.12-1.26); carbon border components are a modest share and are larger in carbon-intensive industries and for countries with high δ~i\tilde{\delta}_iδ~i​.

  • Proposal 1: unilaterally optimal domestic carbon taxes alone cut global CO2_22​ by 5.4%; adding optimal carbon border taxes cuts it by 6.6%, an extra 1.3%. First-best global carbon taxes cut emissions by 41.0% (an extra 37.6% relative to unilateral carbon taxes). Carbon border taxes thus deliver only 1.3/37.6=3.4%1.3/37.6=3.4\%1.3/37.6=3.4% of the cooperative reduction, while lowering global real consumption by 0.5%. First-best taxes cost 0.6% of real consumption but raise climate-adjusted welfare by 1.1%.

  • Border taxes fail for three reasons: (1) they are based on country-industry average carbon intensity, so individual firms have no incentive to abate; (2) they cannot reach nontraded goods, which account for most emissions; (3) they lower world energy demand and pre-tax energy prices, causing leakage (emissions rise in Russia, Saudi Arabia, Canada).

  • Proposal 2: the maximal carbon tax target supporting universal membership is $36/tCO2_22​ with the EU as core (13.4% emissions cut), $53 with EU+US (18.6%), and $89 with EU+US+China (28.0%), the last one achieving 28.0/41.0≈68%28.0/41.0\approx68\%28.0/41.0≈68% of the first-best reduction. With EU+US as core, the club expands over six rounds following the gravity structure of trade (Brazil, Canada, Korea, Turkey first; Australia and India last), i.e. from West to East.

  • Clubs outperform border taxes because penalties are used as an enforcement device that induces domestic carbon taxes (which reach all firms and nontraded goods), and because members pool their market power into harsher penalties.

Extensions

  • Robustness: results are similar with SC-CO2=92_2=922​=92, country-level social costs of carbon, a common trade elasticity, Cobb-Douglas production (ς→1\varsigma\to1ς→1), or a less elastic energy supply (φi/(1−φi)=2\varphi_i/(1-\varphi_i)=2φi​/(1−φi​)=2). An IV-style test following Adao, Costinot and Donaldson (2025), using 2014-2022 carbon tax changes, does not reject the model.

  • Cooperative border taxes (second-best when first-best carbon taxes are politically infeasible) cut global emissions by only 0.9%. If the EU puts weight on foreign welfare, its policy frontier shows that the “externality-free” policy cuts global emissions by 3.4% but raises leakage. Under unilateral policy the EU can at best cut global emissions by about 5%, compared to over 13% in an EU-led club.

  • Increasing returns to scale (Krugman): optimal policy adds a carbon-blind production subsidy si,k∗=1/(γk−1)s_{i,k}^*=1/(\gamma_k-1)si,k∗​=1/(γk​−1), and carbon border taxes are scaled down by (γk−1)/γk(\gamma_k-1)/\gamma_k(γk​−1)/γk​ because output contraction raises emissions per unit.
