Brownian motion, also known as Brownian movement, is the random motion of particles suspended in a fluid (liquid or gas) resulting from their collision with the fast-moving atoms or molecules in the gas or liquid. This phenomenon is named after the Scottish botanist Robert Brown, who first observed it in 1827 while studying pollen particles floating in water under a microscope.

Key Characteristics of Brownian Motion

  • Randomness: The motion is erratic and unpredictable, with particles moving in random directions.

  • Continuous Paths: Particles exhibit continuous but non-differentiable paths.

  • Scale Independence: The motion occurs at microscopic scales and is independent of the size of the particles (as long as they are small enough).

  • Dependence on Temperature: The intensity of Brownian motion increases with temperature, as higher temperatures result in faster-moving fluid molecules.

Mathematical Description

Brownian motion is mathematically described by the Wiener process, a continuous-time stochastic process. Key properties include:

  • Mean Zero: The expected value of the displacement is zero.

  • Variance Proportional to Time: The variance of the displacement increases linearly with time.

  • Independent Increments: Displacements over non-overlapping time intervals are independent.
