Reference: Berry (1992)

Model Setting

The paper studies entry into airline city-pair markets. The central idea is to use observed entry decisions to infer underlying firm profitability while allowing for two features that are essential in oligopoly markets: (1) firms are heterogeneous in their profitability; (2) profitability depends on the number of competing firms that enter.

Consider market iii with KiK_iKi​ potential entrants. Firm kkk chooses whether to enter the market,

sik∈{0,1}(7.4.3.1)s_{ik}\in\{0,1\} \tag{7.4.3.1}sik​∈{0,1}(7.4.3.1)

Let Ni(s)=∑k=1KisikN_i(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{K_i}s_{ik}Ni​(s)=∑k=1Ki​​sik​ denote the number of firms that enter. The post-entry profit of firm kkk is specified as

πik(s)=vi ⁣(Ni(s))+ϕik,(7.4.3.2)\pi_{ik}(s) = v_i\!\left(N_i(s)\right) + \phi_{ik}, \tag{7.4.3.2}πik​(s)=vi​(Ni​(s))+ϕik​,(7.4.3.2)

where vi(N)v_i(N)vi​(N) is the component of profits common to firms in market iii and ϕik\phi_{ik}ϕik​ captures firm-specific profitability.

The key assumption is

vi(N+1)<vi(N).(7.4.3.3)v_i(N+1)<v_i(N). \tag{7.4.3.3}vi​(N+1)<vi​(N).(7.4.3.3)

Thus, additional entry reduces the profitability of all firms. This captures competitive pressure without specifying a complete pricing or quantity-setting game.

The firm-specific term ϕik\phi_{ik}ϕik​ allows different airlines to have different profitability in the same city-pair market. In the airline application, an important source of such heterogeneity is the airline's existing presence at the two endpoint airports.

Entry Equilibrium

A pure-strategy Nash equilibrium requires that every entering firm earns nonnegative profits and every non-entering firm would earn negative profits if it entered.

Because firms differ in ϕik\phi_{ik}ϕik​, order them from most to least profitable:

ϕi1≥ϕi2≥⋯≥ϕiKi.(7.4.3.4)\phi_{i1} \geq \phi_{i2} \geq \cdots \geq \phi_{iK_i}. \tag{7.4.3.4}ϕi1​≥ϕi2​≥⋯≥ϕiKi​​.(7.4.3.4)

The equilibrium number of firms is

Ni∗=max⁡{n:vi(n)+ϕin>0}.(7.4.3.5)N_i^* = \max \left\{ n: v_i(n)+\phi_{in}>0 \right\}. \tag{7.4.3.5}Ni∗​=max{n:vi​(n)+ϕin​>0}.(7.4.3.5)

The intuition is straightforward. If the nnnth most profitable firm can earn positive profits when there are nnn firms in the market, then the market can support at least nnn firms.

Conversely, if the (n+1)(n+1)(n+1)th most profitable firm cannot earn positive profits when there are n+1n+1n+1 firms, the market cannot support n+1n+1n+1 firms.

Uniqueness of the Number of Firms

An important result is that the identities of entering firms need not be unique, but the equilibrium number of firms is unique.

Suppose Ni∗N_i^*Ni∗​ is defined by

vi(Ni∗)+ϕiNi∗>0(7.4.3.6)v_i(N_i^*)+\phi_{iN_i^*}>0 \tag{7.4.3.6}vi​(Ni∗​)+ϕiNi∗​​>0(7.4.3.6)

vi(Ni∗+1)+ϕi,Ni∗+1<0.(7.4.3.7)v_i(N_i^*+1)+\phi_{i,N_i^*+1}<0. \tag{7.4.3.7}vi​(Ni∗​+1)+ϕi,Ni∗​+1​<0.(7.4.3.7)

A similar contradiction rules out an equilibrium with fewer than Ni∗N_i^*Ni∗​ firms.

Hence,

all pure-strategy equilibria have the same number of firms Ni∗(7.4.3.8)\boxed{ \text{all pure-strategy equilibria have the same number of firms }N_i^* } \tag{7.4.3.8}all pure-strategy equilibria have the same number of firms Ni∗​​(7.4.3.8)

This result is important empirically because the econometrician can use the equilibrium number of firms without having to resolve equilibrium multiplicity over firm identities.

Why Maximum Likelihood Is Difficult

In principle, the model could be estimated by calculating

Pr⁡(Ni∗=N∣Xi,Zi,θ)(7.4.3.20)\Pr(N_i^*=N\mid X_i,Z_i,\theta) \tag{7.4.3.20}Pr(Ni∗​=N∣Xi​,Zi​,θ)(7.4.3.20)

for every possible equilibrium number of firms.

The problem is not merely the dimension of the integral. The more serious problem is the complicated region of the unobservables associated with a given equilibrium outcome.

For example, determining the probability that exactly jjj firms are profitable requires considering all possible subsets of jjj firms among KiK_iKi​ potential entrants.

The number of such subsets is

(Kij).(7.4.3.21){K_i\choose j}. \tag{7.4.3.21}(jKi​​).(7.4.3.21)

In the airline data, the number of potential entrants can be as large as 26. For example,

(2611)>7,000,000.(7.4.3.22){26\choose11} > 7,000,000. \tag{7.4.3.22}(1126​)>7,000,000.(7.4.3.22)

Thus, likelihood evaluation may require millions of separate regions of integration, and these calculations must be repeated many times during numerical optimization.

Direct maximum likelihood estimation of the general model is therefore computationally impractical.

Special Cases

Several restrictions produce simpler models.

If

δ=0andρ=0,(7.4.3.23)\delta=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \rho=0, \tag{7.4.3.23}δ=0andρ=0,(7.4.3.23)

one obtains a standard probit model. Firms are heterogeneous, but one firm's entry has no effect on another firm's profitability.

Alternatively, if there is no unobserved firm heterogeneity, firms can be ranked entirely using observed characteristics. The equilibrium number of firms can then be estimated using an ordered-probit-type model.

This model is computationally simple but imposes strong restrictions on which firms can enter.

The general model is designed to allow simultaneously for

strategic interaction+observed heterogeneity+unobserved heterogeneity.(7.4.3.24)\boxed{ \text{strategic interaction} + \text{observed heterogeneity} + \text{unobserved heterogeneity}. } \tag{7.4.3.24}strategic interaction+observed heterogeneity+unobserved heterogeneity.​(7.4.3.24)

Order of Entry

Although the equilibrium number of firms is unique, firm identities may not be unique.

To use information on which airlines actually enter, Berry imposes an order of entry.

Two alternatives are considered.

The first assumes that more profitable firms move first.

The second assumes that incumbents move first. Among incumbents, more profitable firms move first, and among potential entrants, more profitable firms move first.

Conditional on an entry order, the identity of entrants becomes uniquely determined.

The entry-order assumption is therefore mainly needed to exploit information about

who enters,(7.4.3.25)\text{who enters}, \tag{7.4.3.25}who enters,(7.4.3.25)

rather than simply

how many firms enter.(7.4.3.26)\text{how many firms enter}. \tag{7.4.3.26}how many firms enter.(7.4.3.26)

Data

The empirical application uses the U.S. Department of Transportation Origin and Destination Survey.

A market is a city pair. The analysis focuses on pairs formed by the 50 largest U.S. cities, producing approximately 1,200 city-pair markets.

The data compare the first and third quarters of 1980.

A firm is treated as serving a market if it carries a sufficiently large number of passengers between the two cities.

The preferred model estimates

Policy Counterfactual

The importance of strategic interaction becomes especially clear in the policy experiments.

Consider a counterfactual in which all airlines receive access to both endpoint airports and therefore become “well-qualified” potential entrants.

Under the simple probit model, the predicted mean number of firms increases from approximately

1.33to6.33,(7.4.3.49)1.33 \quad\text{to}\quad 6.33, \tag{7.4.3.49}1.33to6.33,(7.4.3.49)

an increase of roughly five firms per market.

Under the full structural model, the predicted mean increases only from

1.70to2.08,(7.4.3.50)1.70 \quad\text{to}\quad 2.08, \tag{7.4.3.50}1.70to2.08,(7.4.3.50)

or approximately

0.4(7.4.3.51)0.4 \tag{7.4.3.51}0.4(7.4.3.51)

additional firms.

The reason is that the probit model treats firms' entry decisions as essentially independent. Making every firm more profitable therefore produces many new entrants.

The structural model instead recognizes that

N↑⟹πik(N)↓.(7.4.3.52)N\uparrow \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \pi_{ik}(N)\downarrow. \tag{7.4.3.52}N↑⟹πik​(N)↓.(7.4.3.52)

Competition therefore offsets much of the direct effect of improved airport access.

Interpretation

The main empirical lesson is not simply that airport presence matters.

The more general lesson is that counterfactual entry predictions require both

firm heterogeneity+strategic interaction.(7.4.3.53)\boxed{ \text{firm heterogeneity} + \text{strategic interaction}. } \tag{7.4.3.53}firm heterogeneity+strategic interaction.​(7.4.3.53)

A model with heterogeneity but no strategic interaction may describe which firms are more likely to enter, but it can severely overpredict the response of market structure to policies that simultaneously affect many potential entrants.

A model with strategic interaction but identical firms, on the other hand, cannot adequately explain which firms enter.

Berry's model combines both mechanisms.

Limitations

The model imposes several important restrictions.

First, rival characteristics affect firm kkk mainly through the number of rivals NNN, rather than through their identities. Thus, the model does not allow

πk(American enters)≠πk(another small airline enters)(7.4.3.54)\pi_k(\text{American enters}) \neq \pi_k(\text{another small airline enters}) \tag{7.4.3.54}πk​(American enters)=πk​(another small airline enters)(7.4.3.54)

holding the total number of competitors fixed.

Second, the airline's broader network is treated as predetermined when the city-pair entry decision is made. The model therefore does not solve the full dynamic airline-network formation problem.

Third, entry-order assumptions are required to use information on entrant identities.

Finally, because the model uses entry decisions rather than a complete model of prices, quantities, demand, and costs, the counterfactuals describe changes in market structure but do not provide a complete welfare analysis.
