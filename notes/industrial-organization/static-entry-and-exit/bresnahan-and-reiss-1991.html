Reference: Bresnahan and Reiss (1991)

Model Setting

The model follows Bresnahan and Reiss (1991) and studies how entry changes competitive conduct in concentrated local markets. The key idea is to infer changes in firms' variable profits from the relationship between market size and the equilibrium number of firms. This approach is particularly useful when firm-level prices, quantities, and price-cost margins are not directly observed.

Consider a local market indexed by iii. Let NNN denote the number of active firms in the market. The profit of the NNNth firm is specified as

ΠiN=SiViN−FiN+εi,(7.4.2.1)\Pi_{iN} = S_i V_{iN} - F_{iN} + \varepsilon_i, \tag{7.4.2.1}ΠiN​=Si​ViN​−FiN​+εi​,(7.4.2.1)

where SiS_iSi​ measures the size of market iii, ViNV_{iN}ViN​ is variable profit per unit of market size when there are NNN active firms, FiNF_{iN}FiN​ is the fixed cost associated with the NNNth entrant, and εi\varepsilon_iεi​ captures unobserved determinants of profitability.

The model therefore decomposes firm profit into three components:

Profit=Market Size×Variable Profit per Consumer−Fixed Cost+Profit Shock.(7.4.2.2)\text{Profit} = \text{Market Size} \times \text{Variable Profit per Consumer} - \text{Fixed Cost} + \text{Profit Shock}. \tag{7.4.2.2}Profit=Market Size×Variable Profit per Consumer−Fixed Cost+Profit Shock.(7.4.2.2)

The empirical application focuses on geographically isolated local markets so that local population provides a meaningful measure of the demand available to firms. In the tire dealer industry, market size is specified as

Si=TPOPi+λ1OPOPi+λ2NGRWi+λ3PGRWi+λ4OCTYi,(7.4.2.3)S_i = TPOP_i + \lambda_1 OPOP_i + \lambda_2 NGRW_i + \lambda_3 PGRW_i + \lambda_4 OCTY_i, \tag{7.4.2.3}Si​=TPOPi​+λ1​OPOPi​+λ2​NGRWi​+λ3​PGRWi​+λ4​OCTYi​,(7.4.2.3)

where TPOPiTPOP_iTPOPi​ is current town population, OPOPiOPOP_iOPOPi​ is nearby population, NGRWiNGRW_iNGRWi​ and PGRWiPGRW_iPGRWi​ capture negative and positive population growth, respectively, and OCTYiOCTY_iOCTYi​ measures the number of residents commuting outside the county.

The coefficient on TPOPiTPOP_iTPOPi​ is normalized to one. This normalization fixes the scale of market size and allows the estimated entry thresholds to be expressed in units comparable to town population.

Fixed Costs

The fixed-cost component is specified as

FiN=γ1+γLLANDVi+∑n=2Nγn,(7.4.2.12)F_{iN} = \gamma_1 + \gamma_L LANDV_i + \sum_{n=2}^{N}\gamma_n, \tag{7.4.2.12}FiN​=γ1​+γL​LANDVi​+n=2∑N​γn​,(7.4.2.12)

where LANDViLANDV_iLANDVi​ is the value of agricultural land and proxies for local differences in fixed operating costs.

For a monopolist,

Fi1=γ1+γLLANDVi.(7.4.2.13)F_{i1} = \gamma_1+\gamma_L LANDV_i. \tag{7.4.2.13}Fi1​=γ1​+γL​LANDVi​.(7.4.2.13)

For subsequent entrants,

Fi2=Fi1+γ2,(7.4.2.14)\begin{aligned} F_{i2} &=F_{i1}+\gamma_2,\end{aligned} \tag{7.4.2.14}Fi2​​=Fi1​+γ2​,​(7.4.2.14)
Fi3=Fi2+γ3,(7.4.2.15)\begin{aligned} F_{i3} &=F_{i2}+\gamma_3,\end{aligned} \tag{7.4.2.15}Fi3​​=Fi2​+γ3​,​(7.4.2.15)
Fi4=Fi3+γ4,(7.4.2.16)\begin{aligned} F_{i4} &=F_{i3}+\gamma_4,\end{aligned} \tag{7.4.2.16}Fi4​​=Fi3​+γ4​,​(7.4.2.16)

Hence, γn\gamma_nγn​ measures the difference in fixed cost between the nnnth and (n−1)(n-1)(n−1)th entrants. Positive values imply that later entrants face higher fixed costs.

Importantly, the model does not separately identify whether these additional fixed costs arise because later entrants are less efficient or because they face entry barriers. Both channels appear in the fixed-cost component of the profit function.

Entry Equilibrium

The econometric model does not directly observe firms' profits. Instead, it uses the equilibrium number of firms in each market to infer whether potential entrants earn positive or negative profits.

Suppose that market iii contains exactly NNN active firms. In equilibrium, the NNNth firm must find entry profitable, while an additional (N+1)(N+1)(N+1)th firm must find entry unprofitable:

ΠiN≥0andΠi,N+1<0.(7.4.2.18)\Pi_{iN}\geq 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \Pi_{i,N+1}<0. \tag{7.4.2.18}ΠiN​≥0andΠi,N+1​<0.(7.4.2.18)

Therefore, observing NNN firms provides information about the latent profits of successive entrants even though profits themselves are not observed.

For example, observing a duopoly implies

Πi2≥0>Πi3.(7.4.2.19)\Pi_{i2}\geq0>\Pi_{i3}. \tag{7.4.2.19}Πi2​≥0>Πi3​.(7.4.2.19)

Similarly, observing a monopoly implies

Πi1≥0>Πi2.(7.4.2.20)\Pi_{i1}\geq0>\Pi_{i2}. \tag{7.4.2.20}Πi1​≥0>Πi2​.(7.4.2.20)

This equilibrium condition is the central identifying restriction of the empirical model.

Ordered Probit Estimation

Define the deterministic component of profits as

ΠˉiN=SiViN−FiN,(7.4.2.21)\bar{\Pi}_{iN} = S_iV_{iN}-F_{iN}, \tag{7.4.2.21}ΠˉiN​=Si​ViN​−FiN​,(7.4.2.21)

so that

ΠiN=ΠˉiN+εi.(7.4.2.22)\Pi_{iN} = \bar{\Pi}_{iN} + \varepsilon_i. \tag{7.4.2.22}ΠiN​=ΠˉiN​+εi​.(7.4.2.22)

The unobserved profit shock is assumed to be normally distributed:

εi∼N(0,1).(7.4.2.23)\varepsilon_i\sim N(0,1). \tag{7.4.2.23}εi​∼N(0,1).(7.4.2.23)

The variance is normalized to one because the overall scale of the latent profit function is not separately identified. The shock is assumed to be independent across markets. Within a market, the same profit shock applies to the profitability of successive entrants.

Let Φ(⋅)\Phi(\cdot)Φ(⋅) denote the standard normal cumulative distribution function. The probability that market iii has no active firm is

Numerical Implementation

For the tire dealer specification, the parameter vector contains 19 parameters:

θ=(λ1,…,λ4, β1,…,β4, α1,…,α5, γ1,…,γ5, γL).(7.4.2.35)\theta = ( \lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_4,\, \beta_1,\ldots,\beta_4,\, \alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_5,\, \gamma_1,\ldots,\gamma_5,\, \gamma_L ). \tag{7.4.2.35}θ=(λ1​,…,λ4​,β1​,…,β4​,α1​,…,α5​,γ1​,…,γ5​,γL​).(7.4.2.35)

We can minimize the negative log likelihood using a constrained nonlinear optimizer. The resulting estimates are then used as starting values for a second constrained optimization step.

Asymptotic standard errors are calculated using the inverse of the numerical Hessian:

Var^(θ^)=H(θ^)−1,(7.4.2.36)\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat{\theta}) = H(\hat{\theta})^{-1}, \tag{7.4.2.36}Var(θ^)=H(θ^)−1,(7.4.2.36)

where H(θ^)H(\hat{\theta})H(θ^) is the Hessian of the negative log likelihood evaluated at the optimum. Parameters that lie on a binding inequality constraint require nonstandard inference and are therefore not assigned conventional Hessian-based standard errors in the replication.

Entry Thresholds

After estimating the profit function, the model uses the zero-profit condition to recover the market size required to support successive entrants.

The entry threshold for the NNNth firm, denoted by SNS_NSN​, satisfies

ΠN=0.(7.4.2.37)\Pi_N=0. \tag{7.4.2.37}ΠN​=0.(7.4.2.37)

Ignoring the mean-zero profit shock and evaluating observable characteristics at representative values, the break-even condition is

SNVN−FN=0.(7.4.2.38)S_NV_N-F_N=0. \tag{7.4.2.38}SN​VN​−FN​=0.(7.4.2.38)

Therefore,

SN=FNVN.(7.4.2.39)S_N = \frac{F_N}{V_N}. \tag{7.4.2.39}SN​=VN​FN​​.(7.4.2.39)

SNS_NSN​ measures the total market size required for the NNNth firm to break even.

Because a market supporting NNN firms is mechanically larger than a market supporting only one firm, the paper defines the per-firm entry threshold as

sN=SNN.(7.4.2.40)s_N = \frac{S_N}{N}. \tag{7.4.2.40}sN​=NSN​​.(7.4.2.40)

This variable measures the amount of market demand required per active firm when the market contains NNN firms.

The main objects of interest are ratios of successive per-firm entry thresholds:

sN+1sN.(7.4.2.41)\frac{s_{N+1}}{s_N}. \tag{7.4.2.41}sN​sN+1​​.(7.4.2.41)

If entry does not change competitive conduct and firms have similar costs, then

sN+1sN=1.(7.4.2.42)\frac{s_{N+1}}{s_N}=1. \tag{7.4.2.42}sN​sN+1​​=1.(7.4.2.42)

In contrast, if entry intensifies competition and reduces variable profit per consumer, the additional firm requires a larger customer base to cover its fixed costs, implying

sN+1sN>1.(7.4.2.43)\frac{s_{N+1}}{s_N}>1. \tag{7.4.2.43}sN​sN+1​​>1.(7.4.2.43)

Thus, departures of successive entry-threshold ratios from one provide information about how competitive conduct changes as the number of firms increases.

Interpretation of Competitive Conduct

The entry-threshold ratios should be interpreted as measures of changes in competition rather than measures of the absolute level of competition.

For example,

s4s3≃1(7.4.2.44)\frac{s_4}{s_3}\simeq1 \tag{7.4.2.44}s3​s4​​≃1(7.4.2.44)

means that moving from three to four firms produces little additional change in firms' variable profits. It does not imply that a three-firm market is perfectly competitive.

The same entry-threshold ratio could in principle arise in a highly competitive market or in a market in which firms maintain a stable degree of market power as additional firms enter. Price or quantity information is therefore useful for determining the absolute level of competition.

For the tire dealer industry, the estimated entry thresholds reported in the paper are approximately

(S1,S2,S3,S4,S5)=(0.49, 1.78, 3.41, 4.74, 6.10).(7.4.2.45)(S_1,S_2,S_3,S_4,S_5) = (0.49,\,1.78,\,3.41,\,4.74,\,6.10). \tag{7.4.2.45}(S1​,S2​,S3​,S4​,S5​)=(0.49,1.78,3.41,4.74,6.10).(7.4.2.45)

The corresponding successive per-firm entry-threshold ratios are

(s2s1,s3s2,s4s3,s5s4)=(1.81, 1.28, 1.04, 1.03).(7.4.2.46)\left( \frac{s_2}{s_1}, \frac{s_3}{s_2}, \frac{s_4}{s_3}, \frac{s_5}{s_4} \right) = (1.81,\,1.28,\,1.04,\,1.03). \tag{7.4.2.46}(s1​s2​​,s2​s3​​,s3​s4​​,s4​s5​​)=(1.81,1.28,1.04,1.03).(7.4.2.46)

The sharp increase in the first two ratios indicates that most of the change in competitive conduct occurs with the entry of the second and third firms. In contrast, the ratios associated with the fourth and fifth firms are close to one, suggesting that additional entry beyond three firms produces relatively little further change in competitive conduct.

The paper also considers the ratio

s5sN,N=1,…,5,(7.4.2.47)\frac{s_5}{s_N}, \qquad N=1,\ldots,5, \tag{7.4.2.47}sN​s5​​,N=1,…,5,(7.4.2.47)

which is used in Figure 4. As NNN increases, this ratio approaches one. The rapid convergence toward one summarizes the central empirical finding that the marginal competitive effect of entry falls quickly with the number of incumbent firms.
