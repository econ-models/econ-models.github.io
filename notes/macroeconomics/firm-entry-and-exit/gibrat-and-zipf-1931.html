Gibrat's Law, also known as the Law of Proportionate Effect, is a principle in economics and business that explains the growth dynamics of firms or cities. It was first proposed by Robert Gibrat in 1931. The law suggests that a firm's or city's growth rate is independent of its size.

Key Points of Gibrat's Law

(1) Proportional Growth: Gibrat's Law states that the growth rate of a firm (or city) is proportional to its current size. Formally,

St+1=St(1+gt)(4.8.1.1)S_{t+1}=S_t(1+g_t) \tag{4.8.1.1}St+1​=St​(1+gt​)(4.8.1.1)

where StS_tSt​ is the size of the firm (or city) at time ttt, gtg_tgt​ is the growth rate, which is assumed to be random and independent of ttt.

(2) Size Independence: Growth rates are assumed to be independent of initial size. This means small firms (or cities) and large firms (or cities) have the same likelihood of experiencing similar growth rates.

(3) Log-Normal Distribution: Under Gibrat's Law, the size of firms (or cities) tends to follow a log-normal distribution over time because the growth rates are multiplicative. Mathematically, the logarithm of the size (ln(S)\text{ln}(S)ln(S)) will have a normal distribution if the law holds.

(4) Stochastic Nature: Growth rates gtg_tgt​ are often modeled as random variables with a mean and variance that do not depend on the size of the firm.

Applications of Gibrat's Law

(1) Firm Growth: The law has been widely used to model firm dynamics. It predicts that larger firms do not necessarily grow slower or faster than smaller firms. Example: The distribution of firm sizes in an industry may follow a log-normal or Pareto distribution as a result of Gibrat's Law.

(2) Urban Economics: In city size studies, Gibrat's Law is applied to explain the growth of cities. It suggests that small and large cities grow at the same expected proportional rate, leading to the well-known Zipf's Law for city sizes.

(3) Income and Wealth Distribution: Gibrat's Law has been used to explain the distribution of personal incomes or wealth under the assumption of proportionate growth in assets or earnings.

Mathematical Representation

If StS_tSt​ represents the size of a firm at time ttt, and the growth rate gtg_tgt​ is a random variable, Gibrat's Law can be expressed as:

St+1=St(1+gt)(4.8.1.2)S_{t+1}=S_t(1+g_t) \tag{4.8.1.2}St+1​=St​(1+gt​)(4.8.1.2)

or

log(St+1)=log(St)+log(1+gt)(4.8.1.3)\text{log}(S_{t+1})=\text{log}(S_t)+\text{log}(1+g_t) \tag{4.8.1.3}log(St+1​)=log(St​)+log(1+gt​)(4.8.1.3)

This implies that the logarithm of the size follows a random walk, and over time, the size distribution becomes log-normal.

Zipf's Distribution

Axtell (2001) explores the distribution of firm sizes in United States. He shows that Zipf distribution characterizes firm sizes: the probability a firm is larger than size sss is inversely proportional to sss (at least in right tail):

Pr(S≥s)=(s0s)α,s≥s0,α>0(4.8.1.4)\text{Pr}(S\geq s)=(\frac{s_0}{s})^{\alpha},\quad s\geq s_0, \quad \alpha>0 \tag{4.8.1.4}Pr(S≥s)=(ss0​​)α,s≥s0​,α>0(4.8.1.4)

That is:

log[Pr(S≥s)]=α  log(s0s)=α[log(s0)−log(s)](4.8.1.5)\text{log}[\text{Pr}(S\geq s)]=\alpha\;\text{log}(\frac{s_0}{s})=\alpha[\text{log}(s_0)-\text{log}(s)] \tag{4.8.1.5}log[Pr(S≥s)]=αlog(ss0​​)=α[log(s0​)−log(s)](4.8.1.5)

Using Census data and COMPUSTAT data in US, Axtell (2001) finds that α=1.06\alpha=1.06α=1.06. The special case of α=1\alpha=1α=1 is known as the Zipf distribution and has somewhat unusual properties insofar as its moments do not exist.
