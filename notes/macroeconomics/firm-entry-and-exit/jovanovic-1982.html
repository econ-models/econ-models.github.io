Reference: Jovanovic (1982)

Jovanovic (1982) proposed the Passive Learning Model of Firm Growth, which suggests that firms do not know their true production efficiency (cost parameter) when entering the market. However, through operational experience, they gradually learn about their own type and decide whether to continue operating or exit the market. This model explains why the firm exit rate declines with age and why surviving young firms tend to grow faster.

This model is a heterogeneous firm model. Each firm entering the market has a fixed but unknown type θ\thetaθ, which is randomly drawn from a distribution upon market entry:

θ∼N(θˉ,1τθ)(4.8.2.1)\theta\sim N(\bar{\theta},\frac{1}{\tau_{\theta}}) \tag{4.8.2.1}θ∼N(θˉ,τθ​1​)(4.8.2.1)

where τθ\tau_{\theta}τθ​ represents the precision (the inverse of variance) of the prior distribution. Everyone knows the overall distribution, but each firm does not know its specific θ\thetaθ.

The cost function of firms is c(q)g(η)c(q)g(\eta)c(q)g(η), where c(q)c(q)c(q) is convex (the cost is marginally increasing). g(η)g(\eta)g(η) serves as a cost "scaling factor", which is related to productivity η\etaη. The function g(⋅)g(\cdot)g(⋅) is monotonically decreasing, meaning that the larger the η\etaη, the lower the cost (indicating that the firm is more efficient). η\etaη is a random ravariable:

η∼N(θ,1τ)(4.8.2.2)\eta\sim N(\theta,\frac{1}{\tau}) \tag{4.8.2.2}η∼N(θ,τ1​)(4.8.2.2)

where η\etaη provides a signal about θ\thetaθ because the firm's true efficiency is fixed (θ\thetaθ remains constant), but η\etaη fluctuates randomly in each period.

The timeline is: (1) firms choose production qqq; (2) firms produce and observe the cost g(η)g(\eta)g(η); (3) firms update the belief on θ\thetaθ; (4) firms decide whether to continue operation or exit the market.

which means firms form an expectation of g(η)g(\eta)g(η) based on historical information Ia\mathbb{I}_aIa​ and determine their output qqq accordingly.

In this model, firms do not know their true productivity θ\thetaθ but can gradually update their beliefs by observing η\etaη. Since the signal follows a normal distribution, the posterior distribution remains normal. Since the posterior distribution is normal, it can be described using two parameters (ηˉ,a)(\bar{\eta},a)(ηˉ​,a) instead of recording all historical signals.

When a firm enters the market, it assumes its productivity θ\thetaθ follows a known prior distribution:

In dynamic optimization problems, a firm's state variable is its posterior belief about its own productivity. Strictly speaking, the full history information set:

Ia={η1,η2,...,ηa}(4.8.2.8)\mathbb{I}_a=\{\eta_1,\eta_2,...,\eta_{a}\} \tag{4.8.2.8}Ia​={η1​,η2​,...,ηa​}(4.8.2.8)

is high-dimensional because it contains all past signals. However, due to the properties of the normal distribution, the posterior distribution can still be fully characterized by two parameters: the mean μ\muμ and the precision τ\tauτ, which reduces the dimensionality. Thus, the firm's learning process is a "two-dimensional state" problem: tracking only ηˉ\bar{\eta}ηˉ​ (signal mean) and aaa (firm age) is sufficient to fully describe the firm's belief updating process. As aaa increases, the firm's estimate of its own productivity becomes increasingly accurate, eventually converging to the true value. If τ\tauτ or τθ\tau_{\theta}τθ​ changes, the firm's learning process becomes more complex. For example, if τ\tauτ increases (indicating higher signal precision), the firm will converge to the true θ\thetaθ more quickly. However, if the distribution of the signal η\etaη changes over time, the firm's learning rule will become more difficult to track. This process explains the Passive Learning mechanism in Jovanovic (1982) and why firm growth rates decline with age—because the magnitude of updates decreases over time.

According to this model, young firms have a less precise estimate of their own productivity θ\thetaθ, which may lead them to underestimate their true productivity, resulting in a higher probability of exit. Older firms have accumulated more observed signals and have a clearer understanding of their θ\thetaθ. They will only exit when their average signal θˉ\bar{\theta}θˉ is very low.

Although this model predicts that young firms have higher growth rates, as firms age: (1) The learning process comes to an end, with xa→θx_a\rightarrow \thetaxa​→θ (belief convergence); (2) Output growth rates gradually stabilize; (3) As a result, the long-term growth rate distribution of all surviving firms tends to converge.

This implies that: (1) In the short run, Gibrat's Law does not hold, since younger firms grow at a higher rate; (2) In the long-run steady state, Gibrat's Law may hold, as all surviving firms exhibit similar growth rates.

This model makes similar predictions to the previous two models: (1) Firm growth is negatively correlated with size; (2) Smaller firms have a higher probability of exit; (3) Cohort size dispersion increases with age.

There are some limitations of this model. Jovanovic (1982) assumes that a firm's productivity θ\thetaθ is fixed and unchanging, and that firms simply learn their true productivity through signals η\etaη. However, in reality, a firm's productivity may change over time. Additionally, in empirical data, we can observe a firm's output, sales, and costs, but we cannot directly observe the firm's internal belief updating process.

Recently, Chen et al. (2023) provides new empirical evidence on the theoretical predictions in Jovanovic (1982). The core idea of Chen et al. (2023) is: if firms are learning about their productivity, then their sales forecasts should become more accurate as they gain experience. Using Japanese firms' production forecast data, Chen et al. (2023) finds that when firms enter a new market, their sales forecast errors tend to be larger. However, over time, as they gain a better understanding of the market, their forecast errors decrease. This study demonstrates that firms indeed learn through experience, supporting Jovanovic (1982)'s hypothesis. This differs from random growth models, because if productivity were purely random rather than driven by a learning process, the decline in sales forecast errors would not systematically follow a time-dependent pattern.
