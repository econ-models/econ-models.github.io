Atalay (2017) extends the classic multi-sector framework (such as the one used in Hulten's theorem) by relaxing the assumption of unitary elasticities of substitution (Cobb-Douglas). To illustrate how this differs from Hulten's approximation, Atalay provides a simplified "special case" solution (Section I.D). Assume: (1) Full capital depreciation and no physical capital in production (α=0\alpha=0α=0); (2) Symmetric input-output linkages (ΓIJM=1/N\Gamma_{IJ}^{M} = 1/NΓIJM​=1/N); (3) Symmetric preference weights.

The term Ψ\PsiΨ captures the direct impact of industry-specific productivity: (1) If elasticities are low (ϵM→0,ϵD→0\epsilon_{M} \to 0, \epsilon_{D} \to 0ϵM​→0,ϵD​→0), Ψ\PsiΨ becomes small; (2) As shown in Equation (6), observed output QtIQ_{tI}QtI​ is driven by both the idiosyncratic shock AtIA_{tI}AtI​ and the aggregate of shocks ∑AtJ\sum A_{tJ}∑AtJ​; (3) When ϵM\epsilon_{M}ϵM​ is low (inputs are complements), the distinction between idiosyncratic and aggregate shocks blurs in the observational data. A shock to one sector propagates strongly to others because they cannot substitute away from the affected input .

Consequently, distinguishing between "common" (aggregate) shocks and "sectoral" shocks depends heavily on ϵM\epsilon_{M}ϵM​. Atalay finds that when ϵM≈0.1\epsilon_{M} \approx 0.1ϵM​≈0.1, sectoral shocks (AtIA_{tI}AtI​) account for 83% of aggregate volatility, whereas imposing ϵM=1\epsilon_{M}=1ϵM​=1 (Hulten's assumption) would attribute only 21% to sectoral shocks.
