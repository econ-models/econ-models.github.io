Model Setting

The paper studies the optimal design of spatially differentiated subsidies for residential solar panels in the U.S. The environmental benefit of a rooftop panel depends on where it is installed: how much sunlight it gets, and which power plants its output displaces at each hour. Current subsidies are set state by state and vary widely (the present value for a 15-panel system ranges from under $6,000 in states with only the federal credit to nearly $29,000 in New Jersey), so they may allocate panels to the wrong places. The paper combines a model of household solar demand with a model of hourly power plant dispatch and pollution damages, estimates it with (1) satellite data on rooftop panels (DeepSolar, Google Project Sunroof), (2) hourly plant-level production and emissions (Open Grid Emissions), and (3) the AP3 air pollution model, and solves for the nationally optimal subsidies.

The model has three blocks: (1) households choose whether to install and how many panels, given electricity prices, installation prices, sunlight and three types of state subsidies; (2) residential solar and nondispatchable plants (wind, solar) produce first, and dispatchable plants respond to the remaining excess load through plant-specific policy functions with capacity and nonnegativity constraints; (3) dispatchable production maps into emissions and damages. A government chooses subsidies in every state to maximize household utility minus environmental damages subject to a budget constraint. The analysis is partial equilibrium and long run: installation prices and electricity prices are fixed, there are no peer effects, and subsidies are constant over time.

Estimation

Data. DeepSolar (2016) gives tract-level numbers of residential systems and panel area (hence panels per system); Google Project Sunroof gives the distribution of suitable rooftop sizes (Nˉi\bar{N}_iNˉi​) and yearly solar potential for 56,940 tracts, combined with NREL SAM hourly profiles by county and 0.5% annual panel degradation to build AitA_{it}Ait​.   • Validation: implied price elasticity of −0.74-0.74−0.74 (vs. −0.65-0.65−0.65 in Gillingham and Tsvetanov 2019); a $1/W rebate in 12 Northeastern states raises installations by 36% (vs. 47% in Crago and Chernyakhovskiy 2017).

  • Plants: hourly dispatchable output fits with R2R^2R2 of 0.99, 0.96 and 0.96 (East, West, Texas), including afternoon ramping and the fuel mix by excess load.

  • Marginal damages are highest in the East, especially RFC (Mid-Atlantic, lower Great Lakes, coal heavy), and rise with excess load in some regions (NPCC: from $67/MWh to $88/MWh between the 10th and 90th percentiles of excess load), while they are flat in the West and Texas.

Counterfactuals

  • Budget-neutral welfare-maximizing subsidies: optimal subsidies are highest in the Mid-Atlantic and lower Great Lakes and lowest in the Northeast and Northwest (clean grids, little sun). Current installations are about 30% too low in the Midwest and South and 80% too high in the Northeast. Moving to the optimum raises annual damages offset from $262.3M to $292.6M (+11.5%), mostly from CO2_22​e. Almost all adjustment is on the extensive margin.

  • Damage-minimizing subsidies: similar geography with more dispersion (from under $4,000 to over $21,000); damages offset +12.6%.

  • Tract-level subsidies: add little beyond optimal state-level subsidies ($292.9M vs. $292.6M).

  • Unconstrained optimum (no budget constraint): the government maximizes

Extensions

  • Households discounting future subsidies at 15% (as in De Groote and Verboven (2019)): the planner uses mostly upfront cost-based subsidies instead of production subsidies, but the geography and total spending are similar.

  • Line losses (endogenous, following Borenstein and Bushnell 2022): solar is more valuable; cost-neutral gains of 11.4% and 12.3%.

  • Transmission constraints: above the 75th percentile of a region's excess load, plants respond only to their own region's load:
