Model Setting

Generation projects that want to connect to the PJM grid go through an interconnection queue: a sequence of up to three engineering studies, each of which updates the project's interconnection cost. Most projects that enter do not complete. A long queue delays the studies (congestion externality), while the reduced-form evidence finds no quantitatively important cost or local output externalities. The paper builds a dynamic equilibrium model in which entry, waiting, withdrawal, completion and queue congestion are jointly determined, and uses it to evaluate study speedups and entry fees/deposits.

Time is discrete, t=1,2,…t=1,2,\dotst=1,2,… in quarters. Each project solves a finite-horizon optimal stopping problem. A potential project arrives at the start of a period and decides whether to pay an entry cost and join the queue. Projects that join receive up to three studies; between studies they pay waiting costs and can withdraw at any time, and each period they also face an exogenous withdrawal probability (failed permitting, expired leases, etc.). The last required study is the terminal study, after which the project decides whether to complete or withdraw. The first or second study can be terminal; the third always is. After a non-terminal first study the project is in Stage 1 (waiting for the second study); after a non-terminal second study it is in Stage 2 (waiting for the third). A project waits at most T=32T=32T=32 quarters; one that reaches TTT receives its terminal study immediately.

Projects do not interact directly. Each project responds to a vector of aggregate variables, the queue status, which determines its study arrival probabilities and its operating revenues. The queue status evolves deterministically because projects are atomistic.

Queue Status and Equilibrium

Each period an exogenous mass of potential projects Nt(x)N_t(x)Nt​(x) arrives. The entering mass then shrinks deterministically through endogenous withdrawals, exogenous withdrawals and completions. Study arrival probabilities and revenues depend on the queue-status vector Kt(e,x)\mathcal{K}_t(e,x)Kt​(e,x), which has three components: (1) the number of queued projects with weakly higher priority (arrived in e~≤e\tilde{e}\le ee~≤e), i.e. the position in the PJM-wide queue; (2) the number of such higher-priority projects in the same TO; (3) the capacity of each fuel type in service at the start of ttt. (1)–(2) affect study arrival; (3) affects r~it\tilde{r}_{it}r~it​ through wholesale prices. Projects take the path {Kt(e,x)}t\{\mathcal{K}_t(e,x)\}_t{Kt​(e,x)}t​ as given.

Optimal strategies are cutoff rules: a project waits if accumulated ξ\xiξ exceeds a threshold that depends on observed study information and characteristics. Under conditions in the paper's appendix, endogenous withdrawals occur only immediately after a study arrives; in periods without a study, only exogenous withdrawals occur, with probability 1−δ(⋅)1-\delta^{(\cdot)}1−δ(⋅).

A queuing equilibrium is a path of queue statuses {Kt(e,x)}t\{\mathcal{K}_t(e,x)\}_t{Kt​(e,x)}t​, strategies, masses and revenues such that: (i) entry, waiting and completion strategies are optimal given beliefs; (ii) beliefs about study arrivals, revenues and the joint distribution of costs and study information are consistent with the queue statuses; (iii) the queue-status path is generated by the entry, withdrawal and completion decisions. It is a fixed point. This is a large-market rational-expectations equilibrium for a nonstationary environment, close to (nonstationary) oblivious equilibrium: each project conditions on its own state and the equilibrium path of aggregate states, rather than on all other projects' states as in a Markov-perfect equilibrium (Ericson and Pakes 1995).

Estimation

The estimation sample is projects entering 2012–2020 (2,709 projects), with actions through 2023Q1. Three steps.

(1) Transitions and exogenous withdrawal, estimated directly from the data and used as beliefs. Study arrival: a hazard model with a probit link in queue position (priority is based only on entry date, and PJM does not speed up or delay studies by profitability, so survivor selection does not bias arrival estimates). Cost transitions: ordered probit over four cost bins; probits for changes in zzz and for whether the second study is terminal. First-study states (c,z)(c,z)(c,z): ordered probit and probits, multiplied assuming conditional independence. Withdrawals in the quarter a study arrives are endogenous; all others are exogenous, modeled as a stage-specific discrete-time hazard (average 0.029 per quarter).

(2) Payoffs, waiting costs and shock distributions by maximum likelihood, taking r~it\tilde{r}_{it}r~it​ and ωit\omega_{it}ωit​ as known. r~it\tilde{r}_{it}r~it​ is built from hourly PJM wholesale prices over a 25-year life discounted at 5% (zero for batteries); ωit\omega_{it}ωit​ is EIA installation cost net of tax credits (ITC: ×0.7\times0.7×0.7 for solar and batteries; PTC value subtracted for wind). The support of (ξS1,ξS2)(\xi^{S1},\xi^{S2})(ξS1,ξS2) is discretized on a 30-point grid on [−10,10][-10,10][−10,10]; the dynamic program is solved for each grid value, study outcome and characteristic, and the likelihood of each project's observed withdrawal and completion decisions integrates over the unobserved heterogeneity.

(3) Entry costs. A pool of potential entrants is built by group (TO, fuel, size, capacity addition): the number of potential entrants per year is the maximum observed annual count in the group, and a 5% random sample of the added non-entrants (514 projects) is kept, implying an 84% average entry probability. Entry values EViEV_iEVi​ are computed from step (2). Identification

  • All parameters are in dollars per MW because interconnection costs, revenues and construction costs enter the payoff in dollars, so no scale normalization is needed.

  • Shock distributions and payoff parameters: identified backwards from the terminal study using continuous variation in interconnection costs. A change in the third-study cost moves the completion threshold dollar for dollar, so completion probabilities trace out the distribution of ξf\xi^fξf. Projects reaching the third study are selected on ξS1+ξS2\xi^{S1}+\xi^{S2}ξS1+ξS2; variation in second-study costs shifts the continuation cutoff and isolates marginal projects, which share the same accumulated heterogeneity. The same logic with first-study costs identifies the distribution of ξS2\xi^{S2}ξS2; first-study withdrawal decisions then identify ξS1\xi^{S1}ξS1 and θ\thetaθ.

  • Waiting costs γS1,γS2\gamma^{S1},\gamma^{S2}γS1,γS2: identified last, from the indifference condition of the project at the continuation cutoff. They measure the per-period opportunity cost of staying active, not only direct spending.

  • Entry costs: threshold crossing EVi≥CiEV_i\ge C_iEVi​≥Ci​. The main variation is the decline in renewable construction costs ω\omegaω over time, which raises the entry values of later cohorts (from $0.129M/MW for the 2012 cohort to $0.336M/MW for 2020) while xentx^{ent}xent contains no calendar-time terms. Projects cannot delay entry (one-time opportunity) or wait inside the queue for costs to fall (studies arrive on PJM's schedule and the terminal study forces a decision).

Counterfactuals

The equilibrium is simulated by iterating on strategies, queue statuses, beliefs and wholesale prices until convergence. Demand for 2021–2028 is set so that as-is prices stay at the 2021 level ($32.0/MWh) and is held fixed across counterfactuals, so wholesale prices respond to added capacity. Entry surplus is defined as:

where kik_iki​ is nameplate capacity in MW.

  • Study speedup: scale QiS1Q_i^{S1}QiS1​ and QiS2Q_i^{S2}QiS2​ (and each underlying qqq) up by 10–50%, capped at one. A 50% speedup raises completed capacity by 17.8 GW (+32%; renewables +10.7 GW), cuts waiting time by 2.2 quarters (−21-21−21%), raises entry by only 5.7 GW (+2.2%, almost all non-renewable), raises entry surplus by $4.4Bn and lowers the long-run wholesale price by 3.8%.   • Option value: a faster queue compresses the dispersion of ξS2\xi^{S2}ξS2 and ξf\xi^fξf, which lowers the option value of waiting. Replacing σ⋅τκ\sigma\cdot\tau^{\kappa}σ⋅τκ with σ⋅(max⁡{1,τ−Δt})κ\sigma\cdot(\max\{1,\tau-\Delta t\})^{\kappa}σ⋅(max{1,τ−Δt})κ, Δt=2.2\Delta t=2.2Δt=2.2, alone lowers completion by 1.4 GW and entry by 4.1 GW; lower waiting costs and discounting account for the other 9.7 GW of entry.

  • Robustness: doubling the non-entrant pool gives +7.4 GW entry but +17.6 GW completion (extra entry adds congestion); an upper-bound 17% fall in the capacity-market price cuts the completion gain to 14.3 GW.

  • Fees and deposits at $20,000/MW: a nonrefundable fee lowers completion by 1.2%, a deposit refunded on completion by 0.2%; a staged deposit (50% refund if withdrawing after the first study) raises it by 0.8%, and budget-neutral deposits (forfeitures rebated to completers) by 3.6% and 4.2%.
