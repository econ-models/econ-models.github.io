Model Setting

The model follows the dynamic oligopoly framework of Ericson and Pakes (1995). There are III incumbent solar panel manufacturers competing in MMM regional markets over discrete time periods. Firms produce an undifferentiated product and compete in quantities in each regional market. Firms share a common discount factor β\betaβ.

Firm iii is characterized by its energy conversion efficiency, ηit\eta_{it}ηit​, which determines its production cost. The industry state is

st=(ht,wt,dt),(8.4.1.1)s_t=(h_t,w_t,d_t), \tag{8.4.1.1}st​=(ht​,wt​,dt​),(8.4.1.1)

where ht=(η1t,...,ηIt)h_t=(\eta_{1t},...,\eta_{It})ht​=(η1t​,...,ηIt​) is the distribution of firms' energy conversion efficiencies, wtw_twt​ is a common input price, and dt=(d1t,...,dMt)d_t=(d_{1t},...,d_{Mt})dt​=(d1t​,...,dMt​) is the vector of market-specific demand states.

Within each period, firms first observe the industry state and private investment shocks. They then compete in the product market and choose whether to invest in improving energy conversion efficiency. Investment outcomes are realized at the beginning of the next period.

Demand and Product Market Competition

Demand in market mmm is static and given by

where QmtQ_{mt}Qmt​ is total solar panel demand, PmtP_{mt}Pmt​ is the market price, and SmtS_{mt}Smt​ is the consumer subsidy. Subsidies shift the demand state dmtd_{mt}dmt​.

Firms compete à la Cournot. Firm iii chooses quantity qimtq_{imt}qimt​ in market mmm to maximize static product-market profits:

max⁡qimt[Pmt(Qmt;dmt)−mc(ηit,wt)]qimt.(8.4.1.3)\max_{q_{imt}} \left[P_{mt}(Q_{mt};d_{mt})-mc(\eta_{it},w_t)\right]q_{imt}. \tag{8.4.1.3}qimt​max​[Pmt​(Qmt​;dmt​)−mc(ηit​,wt​)]qimt​.(8.4.1.3)

πˉi(st).(8.4.1.4)\bar{\pi}_i(s_t). \tag{8.4.1.4}πˉi​(st​).(8.4.1.4)

Marginal costs depend on the firm's energy conversion efficiency and the common input price. Higher energy conversion efficiency reduces the amount of material required to produce a given amount of electricity-generating capacity and therefore lowers production costs.

Consumer subsidies affect innovation by increasing demand and therefore increasing the future profit gain from lowering marginal costs.

Firms follow Markov strategies that depend only on the current industry state and their private shocks. A Markov-perfect Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile ζ=(ζ1,...,ζI)\zeta=(\zeta_1,...,\zeta_I)ζ=(ζ1​,...,ζI​) such that, for every firm iii,

for every state, private shock, and alternative strategy ζi′\zeta_i'ζi′​.

Estimation: Demand and Production Costs

The model is estimated in two broad steps. First, the product-market model, state transitions, and firms' investment policy functions are estimated. Second, these estimates are used to recover the structural cost of investment.

Demand is assumed to be log-linear:

ln⁡Qmt=α0m+αsmarcsinh⁡(Smt)+αpmln⁡Pmt+ϵmtD,(8.4.1.10)\ln Q_{mt} = \alpha_{0m} +\alpha_{sm}\operatorname{arcsinh}(S_{mt}) +\alpha_{pm}\ln P_{mt} +\epsilon^D_{mt}, \tag{8.4.1.10}lnQmt​=α0m​+αsm​arcsinh(Smt​)+αpm​lnPmt​+ϵmtD​,(8.4.1.10)

where αpm\alpha_{pm}αpm​ is the price elasticity of demand and αsm\alpha_{sm}αsm​ captures the effect of consumer subsidies. Price is instrumented using input prices and, in the baseline specification, solar panel prices in other geographic markets.

The estimated demand parameters are used with firms' Cournot first-order conditions to recover marginal costs. Under constant-elasticity demand,

mcimt=Pmt(1+1αpmqimtQmt).(8.4.1.11)mc_{imt} = P_{mt} \left( 1+\frac{1}{\alpha_{pm}}\frac{q_{imt}}{Q_{mt}} \right). \tag{8.4.1.11}mcimt​=Pmt​(1+αpm​1​Qmt​qimt​​).(8.4.1.11)

The relationship between production costs and observed energy conversion efficiency is estimated using

ln⁡(mcimt)=β0+β1ln⁡(η~it)+β2ln⁡(wt)+ϵimtS,(8.4.1.12)\ln(mc_{imt}) = \beta_0 +\beta_1\ln(\tilde{\eta}_{it}) +\beta_2\ln(w_t) +\epsilon^S_{imt}, \tag{8.4.1.12}ln(mcimt​)=β0​+β1​ln(η~​it​)+β2​ln(wt​)+ϵimtS​,(8.4.1.12)

where η~it\tilde{\eta}_{it}η~​it​ denotes observed energy conversion efficiency. The parameter β1\beta_1β1​ maps improvements in observed efficiency into reductions in marginal costs.

Estimation: State Transitions and Investment Policy

The exogenous states, including the common input price and market-specific demand states, are assumed to follow first-order Markov processes:

(wtdt)=R0+R1(wt−1dt−1)+jt.(8.4.1.13)\begin{pmatrix} w_t\\ d_t \end{pmatrix} = R_0 + R_1 \begin{pmatrix} w_{t-1}\\ d_{t-1} \end{pmatrix} +j_t. \tag{8.4.1.13}(wt​dt​​)=R0​+R1​(wt−1​dt−1​​)+jt​.(8.4.1.13)

Because an improvement in a firm's energy conversion efficiency is assumed to occur if and only if the firm invests, observed efficiency changes reveal the binary investment decision xitx_{it}xit​.

The conditional probability of investment is approximated parametrically. The author first uses LASSO to select variables from a flexible function of the firm's own efficiency and the industry state, and then estimates a logit model using the selected covariates:

Pr⁡(xit=1∣ηit,st)=Λ(f~(ηit,st)′μ).(8.4.1.14)\Pr(x_{it}=1|\eta_{it},s_t) = \Lambda\left(\tilde{f}(\eta_{it},s_t)'\mu\right). \tag{8.4.1.14}Pr(xit​=1∣ηit​,st​)=Λ(f~​(ηit​,st​)′μ).(8.4.1.14)

Conditional on investment, the size of the efficiency improvement, νit\nu_{it}νit​, is estimated using a Gamma distribution.

Estimation: Dynamic Investment Costs

The fixed cost of innovation, γ\gammaγ, is estimated using a two-step forward-simulation estimator following Bajari, Benkard and Levin (2007). The ex-ante value function can be written as

Vi(st;ζ)=E[∑τ=0∞βτπi(xi,t+τ,st+τ;ϵi,t+τ)].(8.4.1.15)V_i(s_t;\zeta) = E\left[ \sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty} \beta^\tau \pi_i(x_{i,t+\tau},s_{t+\tau};\epsilon_{i,t+\tau}) \right]. \tag{8.4.1.15}Vi​(st​;ζ)=E[τ=0∑∞​βτπi​(xi,t+τ​,st+τ​;ϵi,t+τ​)].(8.4.1.15)

Because flow payoffs are linear in the structural parameters, the value function can be expressed as

Vi(st;ζ)=Wi(st;ζ)′u,(8.4.1.16)V_i(s_t;\zeta) = W_i(s_t;\zeta)'u, \tag{8.4.1.16}Vi​(st​;ζ)=Wi​(st​;ζ)′u,(8.4.1.16)

where uuu contains the fixed investment cost γ\gammaγ and the scale of the private investment shocks.

The function Wi(st;ζ)W_i(s_t;\zeta)Wi​(st​;ζ) is approximated by forward simulation. Starting from each observed state, future investment decisions are simulated using the estimated investment policy function, efficiency improvements are drawn from their estimated distribution, and future demand and input prices evolve according to the estimated state transition processes. These simulations generate expected discounted product-market profits and investment decisions.

The structural parameter γ\gammaγ is then estimated by pseudo maximum likelihood using the firm's optimal investment decision. Intuitively, γ\gammaγ is identified by comparing the model-implied future profit gains from investment across industry states with the observed frequency of investment in those states. A state in which investment generates large future profits but firms invest infrequently implies a relatively high fixed cost of innovation.
