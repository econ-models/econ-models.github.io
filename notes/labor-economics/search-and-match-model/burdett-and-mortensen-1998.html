Basic Model

Job search models rest upon two basic principles: (1) Labor market competition between employers is the fundamental determinant of wages; (2) Competition is limited by search frictions, meaning any information imperfection on job offers giving employers market power.

The job search framework encompasses two extreme cases: (1) Competitive labor markets: workers can freely force employers into competing, workers get paid their marginal productivity; (2) Monopsonistic labor markets: the cost of finding alternative employers is infinite, firms offer unemployed workers their reservation wages.

Let's go to a basic structure of "partial equilibrium" search models. The basic settings include: (1) Time is continuous. We focus on steady-states; (2) The labor market features a continuum of measure NNN of (infinitely lived and "similar") workers; (3) Workers are either employed or unemployed. They look for a (better) job in both cases; (4) Job search frictions: unemployed (employed) workers sample job offers sequentially at Poisson rate λ0(λ1)\lambda_0(\lambda_1)λ0​(λ1​); (5) Employed workers exogenously lose their job at Poisson rate δ\deltaδ.

Workers' lifetime value of unemployment is denoted by UUU. Assume UUU is the same for all workers for now. Workers' lifetime value of a job is denoted VVV. VVV differs across workers and jobs. However, we assume that there is a complete ordering of job values upon which all workers agree. This implies existence of a scalar job value index yyy such that V(y;⋅)V(y;\cdot)V(y;⋅) is increasing in yyy for all workers.

A job offer is a chance to draw a job value from a continuous sampling distribution with cdf F(⋅)F(\cdot)F(⋅) over [y‾,y‾][\underline{y},\overline{y}][y​,y​]. We treat F(⋅)F(\cdot)F(⋅) as a given for now (We will see later on how it is endogenously determined in equilibrium). Note that F(⋅)F(\cdot)F(⋅) is independent of a worker's employment status. The typical worker faces an optimal stopping problem: "What offers should I accept, given that I can only accept one offer at a time and acceptance (or rejection) is irreversible?" The solution is that workers follow a reservation value policy: (1) If unemployed, accept any offer of y′y^{\prime}y′ such that V(y′;⋅)≥UV(y^{\prime};\cdot)\geq UV(y′;⋅)≥U; (2) If employed with current job value yyy, accept any offer of y′y^{\prime}y′ such that y′>yy^{\prime}>yy′>y.

To the extent possible, workers self-select into better jobs. Anticipating on firm behavior, we assume that V(y‾)≥UV(\underline{y})\geq UV(y​)≥U, so that unemployed workers will accept any job offer.

Estimation

The typical data source used for estimation is a worker panel, including labor force survey data (CPS, European LFS), household panel data (PSID, BHPS, ECHP, NLSY), or workers' side of panelized matched employer-employee data sets.

These data sets often include: (1) Monthly (sometimes even daily) observations of employment status (employed, unemployed, out of the labor force). This permits construction of spell durations from an initial date onward (left-censoring). Because of the (typically) small TTT, this involves a lot of right-censoring, too; (2) (Yearly) observations of wages (and hours of work) for employed workers; (3) Standard individual and job characteristics, like age, sex, education, industry, occupation, part-time, public sector and other; (4) If using MPD: employer identifier and (sometimes) some employer characteristics (value added per worker, capital stock and others).

In a typical data configuration, the analysis sample is then comprised of an initial (stratified) cross-section of employed or unemployed workers, which are followed until their first observed transition (if one exists).
