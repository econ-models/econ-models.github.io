Reference: Abowd, Kramarz and Margolis (1999); Lecture Notes

We can often find that "Similar workers are paid differently", i.e. observed worker/job characteristics fail to explain cross-section wage variance. "Mincer-type" equations are often used by labor economics to describe the relationship between wage and observable characteristics like education and work experience, proposed by Mincer (1974). However, "Mincer-type" equations applied to individual worker data only explain a fraction (typically, less than 50%) of total wage variance.

The recent advent of matched employer-employee panel data (MEE data) has made it possible to estimate wage equations with two-sided unobserved heterogeneity:

where witw_{it}wit​ is individual log wage, xit\boldsymbol{x}_{it}xit​ is a vector of time-varying individual characteristics, αi\alpha_iαi​ is a person fixed effect, ψj\psi_{j}ψj​ is an employer fixed effect and (i,t)→J(i,t)(i,t)\rightarrow J(i,t)(i,t)→J(i,t) is a "matching function" defining worker iii's employer at date ttt.

MEE datasets are matched panel datasets with the following basic structure: (1) One panel of workers with individual index i∈{1,2,...,I}i\in \{1,2,...,I\}i∈{1,2,...,I} and time index t∈{1,2,...,T}t\in \{1,2,...,T\}t∈{1,2,...,T}; (2) One panel of firms with individual index j∈{1,2,...,J}j\in \{1,2,...,J\}j∈{1,2,...,J} and time index t∈{1,2,...,T}t\in \{1,2,...,T\}t∈{1,2,...,T}; (3) A matching function J(i,t)∈{1,2,...,J}J(i,t)\in \{1,2,...,J\}J(i,t)∈{1,2,...,J} defining worker iii's employer at time ttt.

They usually are administrative data (employer payroll reports collected for tax purposes): (1) Matching wage register data with firm accounting data is feasible by availability of firm ID, not always done; (2) Matching with other individual social security/health insurance data done in some countries (Denmark, Norway, Sweden).

The percentage of log wage variance explained by the error component model equation is around 70-80%. No component really dominates in the explanation of log wage variance. There is evidence of unobserved heterogeneity among workers and employers. There is little prima facie evidence of assortative matching.

To what extent do firm effects ψj\psi_jψj​ reflect firm productivity dispersion? Cahuc, Postel-Vinay and Robin (2006) use (French) wage register data matched with firm accounting data and regress log wages witw_{it}wit​ on employer's mean log productivity (measured by mean log value-added per worker):

The law of one price seems violated in labor markets. Firm heterogeneity is quantitatively important: Low-paying employers survive along with high-paying employers.
